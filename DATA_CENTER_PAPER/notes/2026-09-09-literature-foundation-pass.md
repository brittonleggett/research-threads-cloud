## Addendum (2026-09-09, same night) — closing out the four "not done" items

**1. Guidi & Dominici (2026), arXiv:2607.02531 — full text reached.** Maps water consumption
across 472 U.S. hyperscale data centers by linking facility locations to electricity regions,
hydrologic basins, and water-stress data, tracking two pathways: direct on-site cooling and
water consumed in electricity generation. Finding: ~300 GL/year total operational water
consumption; electricity-related water is three-quarters of the total (bigger than direct
cooling). Direct-cooling burdens concentrate in western/south-central water-stressed basins;
electricity-related burdens cluster in eastern grid regions dependent on fossil generation — just
3 of 24 hosting balancing authorities account for 59% of electricity-related water. **No Louisiana-
specific mention found** in the reachable content — the paper's framing is national/regional
(balancing-authority level), not state-by-state, so it doesn't directly cite Richland Parish/Meta
"Hyperion," but the general finding (electricity-related water often exceeds direct-cooling water,
concentrated in fossil-heavy grid regions) is a real, usable framing point for that case.

**2. Cartwright (2026), *Climate and Energy* 42, 15-17, DOI 10.1002/gas.70012 — still not reached,
confirmed genuine access wall.** Wiley 403'd again on direct fetch. Confirmed via search: a short
3-page piece (not a full research article) by Echo D. Cartwright, arguing environmental-justice
impacts of data centers are under-discussed relative to energy/grid/water framing. DeepDyve lists
it (a paywall rental service, not open access). Treat as citable-by-title/abstract only unless
Britton pulls it via his own library access, same pattern as tonight's CCS pulls.

**3. The EJI/social-vulnerability finding disambiguated as real but NOT the same as Ancona et al.
— these are two separate, both-real studies, not one.**
- **Ancona et al. (2026), "Urban infrastructure and fossil fuel industrial legacy drive US data
  center siting," *Nature Cities*, DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00487-z** — confirmed as a real, distinct
  paper: 4,283 commercial data centers (2025 Data Center Map database), headline finding is that
  97.5% of facilities sit in metro/micropolitan areas (challenging "rural cloud expansion"
  narratives) and that urban infrastructure + fossil-fuel industrial legacy predict siting, not an
  EJI-specific framing. Full text not reached (Nature login-wall redirect).
- **A separate, real finding — still not pinned to one single confirmed primary source after this
  pass, genuinely unresolved, not just under-searched:** a "~700 data centers nationally, nearly
  half in census tracts with above-median CDC Environmental Justice Index burden" claim recurs
  across secondary sources. The closest candidate found is **George, B. (2026), "Health
  Consequences of Large Data Centers: Air Pollution, Noise, Water Use, and Environmental Justice,"
  SocArXiv preprint** (Alcorn State University) — abstract-level only reached, modeling ~1,300
  premature deaths/year and ~600,000 asthma cases/year nationally from data-center-linked air
  pollution by the late 2020s, ~$20B annual public health cost, disproportionate impact on
  vulnerable communities — but the abstract does NOT contain the specific "~700 facilities/half
  above-median EJI" figure, so this may or may not be the source of that claim. **Flag clearly: do
  not cite the "~700/half" EJI figure to either Ancona or George without further verification** —
  it remains a real, recurring claim with an unconfirmed primary source.

**4. Next10 (California policy-research org), "The Intersection of Data Center Development, Water
Availability, and Environmental Justice In California" — found and read.** Not peer-reviewed (policy
report), but substantive: built a GIS-based vulnerability index combining water-scarcity and social-
vulnerability indicators across California data centers, with 5 case-study facilities. Key finding
directly useful as a comparison case for Louisiana's own Richland Parish/Meta "Hyperion" water-use
concern: newer hyperscale facilities are increasingly sited in regions with *higher* social
vulnerability and water scarcity than earlier urban tech-hub locations, with Imperial County and
Sacramento flagged as the sharpest water-scarcity/social-vulnerability intersection points, and a
real regulatory-gap finding — some data centers may avoid full CEQA environmental/water review.

## 1. Transferable literature already verified tonight in the CCS Paper thread

CCS's `Conceptual_Model_and_Theory_v2_DRAFT_2026-09-08.md` and its lit-review digest assembled a
real, DOI-verified theoretical base this session. Assessment of what transfers to Data Center
Paper directly vs. needs its own grounding:

**Transfers cleanly (general infrastructure-siting-opposition theory, not CCS-specific):**
- Energy justice triumvirate (McCauley, Heffron, Stephan & Jenkins 2013; Jenkins et al. 2016) —
  distributive/procedural/recognition justice applies to any contested infrastructure siting, not
  just energy. Directly usable.
- Procedural justice/legitimacy theory (Tyler 2003) — same, general theory of governance
  legitimacy, not domain-specific.
- Social license to operate (Prno & Slocombe 2012; Moffat & Zhang 2014; Minadakis & Vega-Araújo
  2024's SLO/EJ synergy review) — SLO literature already spans mining, renewables, and now CCS;
  data centers are a plausible fourth domain, though no direct precedent yet confirmed (see
  Section 2).
- Social acceptance triangle (Wüstenhagen, Wolsink & Bürer 2007) — general framework, transfers.
- NIMBY-critique literature (Wolsink 2006; Devine-Wright 2009 on place-protective action) —
  directly relevant to Data Center's own already-identified "aesthetic/NIMBY framing in wealthy VA
  exurbs" theme; this literature explicitly argues against dismissing such opposition as mere
  self-interest, which fits Data Center's own multi-state comparative design well.
- Arnauld de Sartre & Chailleux (2026) on manufactured "lack of social acceptance" narratives —
  **directly relevant to Data Center's own institutional-secrecy/NDA theme.** New Orleans' actual
  moratorium vote cited secrecy concerns explicitly; this citation gives a real, peer-reviewed
  framework for how industry/government coalitions construct "opposition" narratives to mask other
  failure causes — worth testing whether Data Center's own corpus shows a parallel pattern.

**Does NOT transfer — needs domain-specific grounding:** anything CCS-specific (subsurface
geology, EOR/storage conflation, Indigenous consultation on tar sands/CCS specifically). Data
Center Paper needs its own equivalent grounding, not a copy-paste of CCS's Indigenous-rights
material, since the underlying real-world pattern (NDA secrecy, water competition) is different in
kind, not just in industry label.

## 2. Fresh search: data-center/digital-infrastructure-siting-specific literature

**Found, real, worth pursuing further:**

- **Cartwright, E.D. (2026). "The Environmental Justice and Community Impacts of Data Centers."
  *Climate and Energy*, 42, 15-17. DOI: 10.1002/gas.70012 (Wiley).** Confirmed real via Wiley
  Online Library listing. **Caveat: this is a short (3-page) piece in a trade/policy bulletin, not
  a full empirical research article** — full text not reached (403), so its actual content/rigor
  is not yet verified beyond the title and journal. Worth a follow-up pull via Britton's library
  access before treating it as a substantive citation rather than a pointer.
- A national correlational finding surfaces repeatedly in secondary sources (blog posts, NGO
  reports) but its primary citation was **not pinned down in this pass**: data centers aren't
  disproportionately in high-EJI-score tracts overall, but nearly half sit in census tracts with
  above-median social-vulnerability scores. This may be the same finding as Ancona et al. (2026,
  *Nature Cities*) already cited in this project's own memory as its national EJ-siting anchor —
  or it may be a distinct, newer study. **Needs disambiguation before citing** — don't assume
  it's Ancona et al. without checking.
- No other peer-reviewed academic article specifically on data-center-siting opposition was found
  in this pass. Most real content on this topic currently lives in NGO reports (National Wildlife
  Federation, Climate Justice Alliance, NAACP's "Stop Dirty Data Centers" campaign, Consumer
  Federation of America) and trade/legal press (BRG's ThinkSet, law-firm client alerts) rather than
  peer-reviewed journals — **this is itself worth noting as a real finding, not a search failure**:
  data center opposition is new enough as a public controversy that the academic literature has
  not caught up to it the way CCS/mining/renewable-siting literature has had decades to develop.
  This is a genuine novelty opportunity for the paper, not just a gap to apologize for.

## 3. Water-scarcity/resource-competition literature — Data Center's most distinctive angle

**Real, substantive find:** **Guidi, G., & Dominici, F. (2026). "The Hidden Water Geography of
U.S. Hyperscale Data Centers in the AI Era." arXiv:2607.02531 [cs.CY].** Read in reasonable detail
(abstract-level, full PDF too large to fetch in this pass). A real quantitative geospatial study
of 472 U.S. hyperscale facilities, distinguishing direct cooling water use from indirect
electricity-generation water use. Key findings: total operational water consumption ~300 GL/year;
electricity-related water is three-quarters of the total; **direct cooling impacts concentrate in
stressed western and south-central water basins** (directly relevant to any Western-state cases
in Data Center's own multi-state design), while electricity-related water burdens concentrate in a
few eastern grid regions reliant on fossil fuels; just 3 of 24 hosting balancing authorities
account for 59% of electricity-related water use — a real, citable geographic-inequality finding.
**Caveats:** this is a preprint (arXiv, not yet peer-reviewed) by a co-author (Francesca Dominici)
who is a well-established Harvard biostatistics/environmental-health researcher, which adds real
credibility despite preprint status. The abstract does not itself address environmental justice or
disadvantaged communities directly — that linkage would need to be made by pairing this paper's
geographic water-stress data with separate demographic/EJ data, not assumed from this paper alone.

Also surfaced but not independently verified this pass: a Next10 report specifically on
California data centers, water availability, and environmental justice — real-sounding and
on-point, worth a follow-up read.
