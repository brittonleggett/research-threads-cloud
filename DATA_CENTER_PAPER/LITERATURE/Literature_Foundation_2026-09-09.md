# Data Center Paper — Academic Literature Foundation (first pass, 2026-09-09)

## Why this file exists
This project has been building real-world corpus material (news, court dockets, government
press releases) for nearly a month with **no academic-literature folder at all** — a gap that
matches Britton's 2026-09-08 standing concern about paper threads being citation-chased rather
than corpus-built (see repo memory: literature comes before locking any theory). This is a first,
real search-and-verify pass, not exhaustive — every citation below was independently confirmed
via at least two sources (the publisher/journal page plus a secondary indexer like ResearchGate,
Semantic Scholar, or IDEAS/RePEc) before being included. Nothing here is AI-search-synthesized
without that cross-check, given the fabricated-case-number/date failure mode already caught twice
this week elsewhere in this pipeline.

## Five verified anchor citations

1. **Shi, K., & Dou, P. (2025). "From conflict to resilience: a cross-cultural analysis of NIMBY
   phenomena in urban infrastructure development and its implications for China's new
   urbanization." *Journal of Asian Architecture and Building Engineering.*** (DOI:
   10.1080/13467581.2025.2551971) — a systematic review of 129 NIMBY studies (2010-2024) across
   Asian, European, and American scholarship. Key finding directly useful here: the review
   characterizes **American NIMBY scholarship specifically as integrating community empowerment
   and spatial justice**, distinct from Asian governance-efficiency framing and European
   place-attachment/institutional-equity framing — a citable, general anchor for why this
   project's US corpus should expect empowerment/justice framing to dominate, and a jumping-off
   point for backward-snowballing the American-scholarship studies it reviews.

2. **Hess, D. J., & Satcher, L. A. (2019). "Conditions for successful environmental justice
   mobilizations: an analysis of 50 cases." *Environmental Politics.*** — a QCA-style comparative
   analysis of 50 US environmental-justice cases, coding five mobilizing strategies (local/state
   government coalitions, federal attention, civil disobedience, litigation, national NGO
   support) and three structural conditions (absence of industry counter-mobilization, water
   pollution presence, new-vs-existing siting) against high- vs. low-remediation outcomes (33
   high, 17 low). **Directly maps onto this corpus's own case variation** — Coweta County's
   zoning-based denial, DeKalb's moratorium-extension-via-resident-pressure, Sabey/Decatur's
   stalled litigation, Imperial County's struck-down-then-redrafted moratorium — as a ready-made
   comparative framework for *why* some communities' opposition succeeds and others don't, rather
   than treating every case as a generic "win" or "loss."

3. **Feng, D., Liu, Y., & Ge, Y. (2024). "Social License to Operate for NIMBY Infrastructures:
   The Mechanism of the Four Components of Procedural Justice." *Buildings*, 14(8), 2465.**
   (open access, DOI: 10.3390/buildings14082465) — tests how four procedural-justice components
   (formal/informal treatment by government and by the construction enterprise) drive community
   trust and social acceptance (the "social license to operate," a construct from extractive
   industries now applied to infrastructure siting generally). Finding: the **enterprise's own
   informal treatment of the public** had the largest effect, larger than the government's formal
   process — a citable mechanism for why corporate communication/engagement quality (not just
   the regulatory process) predicts whether opposition escalates, which is the paper's likely
   corporate-response angle.

4. **Calvano, L. (2008). "Multinational Corporations and Local Communities: A Critical Analysis
   of Conflict." *Journal of Business Ethics*, 82(4), 793-805.** — a framework for
   corporation-community conflict built on three factors: stakeholder power inequality,
   stakeholder perception gaps, and cultural/local context. Useful as the theoretical anchor for
   why the *same* company (e.g., Meta, Amazon) produces different outcomes across different
   communities in this corpus — the framework predicts conflict severity from the power/perception
   gap, not from the company or project type alone.

5. **Klein, T. A. (2008). "Assessing Distributive Justice in Marketing: A Benefit-Cost Approach."
   *Journal of Macromarketing*, 28(1), 33-43.** — the most directly marketing-native citation
   found this pass: treats distributive justice (fair apportioning of benefits and costs) as a
   macromarketing/social-performance criterion, proposing benefit-cost analysis as the evaluative
   framework. **Best-fit theoretical anchor for the "diffuse benefits, local costs" framing already
   in this project's own real-world grounding notes** (data centers' benefits are broad/diffuse,
   negative externalities are local) — this citation gives that observation a real, citable
   macromarketing pedigree rather than being this project's own unsupported framing.

## How these map onto the project's existing (still unlocked) theory candidates
- The three regional framings already observed in the 2026-08-16 national scan (racialized
  distributive-injustice in LA/GA, resource-scarcity in the West, aesthetic/NIMBY in wealthy VA
  exurbs) now have real theoretical homes: **distributive injustice → Klein (2008) and the
  distributive-justice half of Hess & Satcher (2019)'s conditions; resource-scarcity → not yet
  covered by these five, worth a dedicated follow-up search; aesthetic/NIMBY → Shi & Dou (2025)'s
  American-scholarship spatial-justice/empowerment framing.**
- **Corporate-response variation** (which this corpus's Study 1 thematic analysis of
  corporate/government statements will presumably code) has two ready theoretical lenses: Calvano
  (2008)'s power/perception-gap framework and Feng, Liu & Ge (2024)'s procedural-justice → social
  license mechanism, with the latter's specific finding (informal enterprise treatment > formal
  government process) a strong candidate for a Study 1 theme or Study 2 manipulated factor.
- **Cross-case outcome variation** (why Coweta's zoning objection succeeded, DeKalb's ordinance
  effort stalled, Sabey's litigation is stuck, Imperial County's moratorium got struck down then
  redrafted) now has a real comparative-case methodology precedent in Hess & Satcher (2019),
  including a specific set of codeable conditions this project could replicate or adapt.

## What this pass did NOT cover (flagged for the next literature pass, not a decision)
- Resource-scarcity/water-conflict framing specifically (the Western-states angle: Imperial
  County, Arizona) has no dedicated citation yet — worth a targeted search on water-stress/
  drought-conflict and infrastructure siting.
- No AI-specific or "hyperscale data center" backlash academic literature was found distinct from
  the general NIMBY/EJ base — expected, since this is a very recent (2024-2026) phenomenon; trade
  press (BRG, Data Center Frontier, Data Center Dynamics) already cited in this project's notes is
  currently the only data-center-specific source material, which is a real, worth-flagging gap
  rather than a search failure.
- This is a first pass by one research session — per Britton's standing "always pull more lit"
  rule, treat this as a floor, not a ceiling; more backward/forward snowballing from these five
  anchors (especially Hess & Satcher's own 50-case dataset and citing/cited-by chains) is
  straightforward future work.

## Design-lock note
None of this constitutes or implies a theory-chain lock — that decision (which framing anchors
the paper, whether region/community-profile becomes a formal moderator) remains Britton's call
per the repo README. This file exists to make sure that decision, whenever he makes it, has real
literature underneath it rather than being made citation-free.
