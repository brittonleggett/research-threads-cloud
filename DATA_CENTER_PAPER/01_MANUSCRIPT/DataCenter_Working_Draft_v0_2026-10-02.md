## 2.1 Candidate theoretical frames (from the 2026-08-12 orientation; NOT locked)

<!-- SOURCE: notes\2026-08-12-orientation.md, lines 43-85. Carried verbatim; only change = bracketed citation-status markers inserted after citations. -->
## Candidate theoretical frames (pick one primary chain, not all of these)

The recurring shape in Britton's other work is antecedent → psychological
mediator → downstream outcome, with a moderator that reshapes the relationship
(see `user_research_corpus.md`). Several candidates fit that same shape here:

- **Environmental justice / distributive justice** — is the burden (water,
  energy, land, noise, health risk) falling disproportionately on communities
  that already carry other industrial burdens? Directly evidenced in the New
  Orleans East case, not a theoretical reach.
- **Procedural justice** — were residents informed and consulted before the
  deal was finalized, or presented with a fait accompli under NDA? The
  secrecy angle is the strongest, most concrete manipulable variable across
  all three LA cases.
- **Psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966) [UNVERIFIED: book, not read]** — same construct already in the
  Tariff Paper's frame; applies naturally to an outside corporation imposing
  a resource-intensive facility without community consent.
- **Risk perception / psychometric paradigm (Slovic) [UNVERIFIED: no specific work named]** — "dread risk" (health,
  environmental harm) and "unknown risk" (opaque AI/data-center technology)
  both plausible drivers of opposition independent of actual harm.
  distinguishes emotional/dread-based opposition from analytic/cost-based
  opposition — could be a useful mediator split.
- **Resource scarcity / commons dilemma** — water and grid capacity framed as
  shared, finite local resources being appropriated by an outside firm.
- **Place attachment / solastalgia** — disruption to community identity and
  sense of place, especially relevant to the New Orleans East case's framing
  of "deep roots" in the neighborhood.

**Strongest candidate primary model** (mirrors the Tariff Paper's structure
closely enough to reuse a lot of the same instrument-development playbook):

Framing of the announcement (transparent/community-consulted vs.
secretive/imposed) **→** perceived procedural injustice / distributive
unfairness **→** trust in company and/or local government **→** opposition
intention (support for moratorium, protest/activism intention, political
voting intention), **moderated by** community environmental-justice history
(prior industrial burden) or by perceived necessity (AI/tech is seen as
inevitable vs. optional).

This is a proposal, not a lock — flagging it because it would let Study 2
reuse much of the Tariff Paper's measurement/analysis approach (fairness →
trust → downstream intention chain, PLS-SEM), which is presumably part of why
"milking all three threads similarly" appeals.

## Five verified anchor citations

1. **Shi, K., & Dou, P. (2025). [VERIFIED (article read 2026-10-02); description broadly accurate, caveats in INTEGRITY FLAG below] "From conflict to resilience: a cross-cultural analysis of NIMBY
   phenomena in urban infrastructure development and its implications for China's new
   urbanization." *Journal of Asian Architecture and Building Engineering.*** (DOI:
   10.1080/13467581.2025.2551971) — a systematic review of 129 NIMBY studies (2010-2024) across
   Asian, European, and American scholarship. Key finding directly useful here: the review
   characterizes **American NIMBY scholarship specifically as integrating community empowerment
   and spatial justice**, distinct from Asian governance-efficiency framing and European
   place-attachment/institutional-equity framing — a citable, general anchor for why this
   project's US corpus should expect empowerment/justice framing to dominate, and a jumping-off
   point for backward-snowballing the American-scholarship studies it reviews.

2. **Hess, D. J., & Satcher, L. A. (2019). [VERIFIED] "Conditions for successful environmental justice
   mobilizations: an analysis of 50 cases." *Environmental Politics.*** — a QCA-style comparative
   analysis of 50 US environmental-justice cases, coding five mobilizing strategies (local/state
   government coalitions, federal attention, civil disobedience, litigation, national NGO
   support) and three structural conditions (absence of industry counter-mobilization, water
   pollution presence, new-vs-existing siting) against high- vs. low-remediation outcomes (33
   high, 17 low). **Directly maps onto this corpus's own case variation** — Coweta County's
   zoning-based denial, DeKalb's moratorium-extension-via-resident-pressure, Sabey/Decatur's
   stalled litigation, Imperial County's struck-down-then-redrafted moratorium — as a ready-made
   comparative framework for *why* some communities' opposition succeeds and others don't, rather
   than treating every case as a generic "win" or "loss."

3. **Feng, D., Liu, Y., & Ge, Y. (2024). [VERIFIED (article read 2026-10-02); the carried description is only partly accurate, see INTEGRITY FLAG below] "Social License to Operate for NIMBY Infrastructures:
   The Mechanism of the Four Components of Procedural Justice." *Buildings*, 14(8), 2465.**
   (open access, DOI: 10.3390/buildings14082465) — tests how four procedural-justice components
   (formal/informal treatment by government and by the construction enterprise) drive community
   trust and social acceptance (the "social license to operate," a construct from extractive
   industries now applied to infrastructure siting generally). Finding: the **enterprise's own
   informal treatment of the public** had the largest effect, larger than the government's formal
   process — a citable mechanism for why corporate communication/engagement quality (not just
   the regulatory process) predicts whether opposition escalates, which is the paper's likely
   corporate-response angle.

4. **Calvano, L. (2008). [UNVERIFIED: paywalled, not read; see MISSING_PDF_LIST] "Multinational Corporations and Local Communities: A Critical Analysis
   of Conflict." *Journal of Business Ethics*, 82(4), 793-805.** — a framework for
   corporation-community conflict built on three factors: stakeholder power inequality,
   stakeholder perception gaps, and cultural/local context. Useful as the theoretical anchor for
   why the *same* company (e.g., Meta, Amazon) produces different outcomes across different
   communities in this corpus — the framework predicts conflict severity from the power/perception
   gap, not from the company or project type alone.

5. **Klein, T. A. (2008). [UNVERIFIED: paywalled, not read; see MISSING_PDF_LIST] "Assessing Distributive Justice in Marketing: A Benefit-Cost Approach."
   *Journal of Macromarketing*, 28(1), 33-43.** — the most directly marketing-native citation
   found this pass: treats distributive justice (fair apportioning of benefits and costs) as a
   macromarketing/social-performance criterion, proposing benefit-cost analysis as the evaluative
   framework. **Best-fit theoretical anchor for the "diffuse benefits, local costs" framing already
   in this project's own real-world grounding notes** (data centers' benefits are broad/diffuse,
   negative externalities are local) — this citation gives that observation a real, citable
   macromarketing pedigree rather than being this project's own unsupported framing.
