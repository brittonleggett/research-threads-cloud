decides — but he does need to decide before the Study 2 section of this package is truly

## Question 6 — Exempt/Expedited Review & Rationale

**Study 1** does not constitute human subjects research under 45 CFR 46.102(e) and is excluded
from IRB oversight. Analyzes only publicly available news coverage, government audit findings,
court filings, and public meeting records — material already made public by news organizations,
courts, and government bodies for general audiences. No individual private data, no human
contact. Secondary basis if preferred: 45 CFR 46.104(d)(4).

**Pretest and Study 2** both qualify for exemption under **45 CFR 46.104(d)(3): Benign Behavioral
Interventions**.
- Adult participants (18+) who prospectively agree to participate.
- Benign behavioral interventions: reading a brief, realistic vignette about a city adopting an
  ALPR camera network (Study 2) or a short set of scale-wording/comprehension items (Pretest), and
  answering standard attitude/perception survey items. Brief, not physically invasive, no
  reasonable expectation of significant or lasting adverse psychological impact.
- No identifying information collected beyond what Question 8a/Confidentiality specify; panel
  platform IDs used only for payment verification, stored separately, de-linked after payment.
- Mild incomplete disclosure (participants aren't told the specific hypotheses in advance, and the
  Study 2 vignette's two conditions aren't described to each other's respondents) remediated by a
  debriefing statement at the end of each survey (see below).
- **If Path A (archival Moderator 1) is adopted**, the ZIP/tract-linkage step should be reviewed

  this explicitly for the board's own read rather than asserting it clears exemption without
  qualification — same honesty standard the rest of this project holds to.

## Question 7 — Abstract

This research program examines how disclosure of a surveillance technology's actual data-sharing
practice — specifically, automated license-plate-reader (ALPR) camera networks operated by Flock
Safety and used by local police departments — shapes public perceptions of procedural injustice,
institutional trust, and opposition intention. Motivated by a documented, multi-state pattern of
ALPR camera networks whose federal/out-of-state data-sharing defaults were not disclosed to, or
understood by, the local officials and communities deploying them (see Study 1 below), the
research draws on procedural justice and police legitimacy theory (Tyler, 1990; Sunshine & Tyler,
2003; Reisig, Bratton, & Gertz, 2007) and Nissenbaum's (2010) theory of privacy as contextual
integrity to propose that undisclosed default data-sharing functions as a specific, procedural
form of injustice — distinct from a generic privacy-concern measure — that erodes institutional
trust and drives opposition to the technology.

The program encompasses two sequential human-subjects phases plus a prior, non-human-subjects
Study 1. **Study 1** (excluded from human-subjects review; public document/news-coverage analysis
only) employs AI-assisted thematic analysis of 22 real, sourced ALPR controversy artifacts across
fourteen U.S. states, producing the typology and theoretical mechanism tested below. A **Pretest**
(target N=150-200 [placeholder — not yet power-analyzed], online panel) validates adapted scale
wording (drawn from Reisig, Bratton, & Gertz, 2007; Miethe, Dudinskaya, Forepaugh, & Sousa, 2025;
van Zomeren, Spears, Fischer, & Leach, 2004 — see `2026-08-16-scale-sourcing.md` for full sourcing)
and the disclosure-condition manipulation before fielding. **Study 2** is a between-subjects online
vignette experiment (target N=500-800 [placeholder, see Question 8b]) in which adult U.S.
residents (18+) are randomly assigned to read a vignette describing a city adopting an ALPR camera
network under either a transparent, disclosed, local-only data-sharing policy or a secret,
undisclosed, broad-access default. Participants then complete measures of perceived procedural
injustice (adapted Quality of Decision Making subscale), institutional trust (adapted Trust in
Police subscale), perceived crime-solving necessity, and opposition intention (adapted collective-
action-tendencies scale), plus manipulation-check, confound-check, and standard demographic items.
No vulnerable populations will be recruited. All data collection occurs online. Findings are
intended for submission to a public-policy/public-administration research outlet (see Question 1).

**AI-use disclosure:** portions of this research program's literature grounding, Study 1 thematic
analysis, theoretical model development, and instrument adaptation were completed with AI
assistance (Claude, Anthropic), under an explicit researcher-granted exception for this project

## Confidentiality

No direct identifiers collected (no name, email, phone, IP address, or precise geolocation — survey
platform configured to suppress IP/location metadata beyond what Path A explicitly requires, if
chosen). Panel participant IDs used only to confirm completion and process payment, stored in a
file separate from survey response data, permanently de-linked and deleted after payment
confirmation.

**If Path A (archival Moderator 1) is adopted:** each respondent's self-reported ZIP or census
tract is a quasi-identifier and must be stored separately from any other potentially identifying
combination of responses, retained only as long as needed to compute the disparity-exposure score,
and reported in the manuscript only in aggregate/binned form (e.g., disparity-score quartile), never
as a raw ZIP tied to an individual response record in any analysis file that leaves the research
team. This detail needs the PI's and, likely, the board's explicit attention before Path A is
finalized — flagged, not resolved, by this draft.

Data stored on password-protected, university-approved survey infrastructure during collection;
exported and stored on a password-protected computer accessible only to the research team after
collection. Retained a minimum of three years post-publication per standard APA/field guidelines,
then securely deleted. All findings reported in aggregate only.

## Voluntary Participation / Informed Consent

Electronic informed consent at the start of each survey (~8th-grade reading level): study purpose
(stated at a general level — "public perceptions of surveillance camera policy," not the specific
disclosure-condition/trust hypotheses), what participation involves, time commitment,
risks/benefits, confidentiality, compensation, right to withdraw. "I agree to participate" / "I do
not agree to participate" radio buttons; non-consenting respondents redirected out immediately, no
penalty. Participants may withdraw at any point before final submission by closing the browser.
Mild incomplete disclosure (specific hypotheses not revealed in advance; Study 2 respondents aren't
told the other condition exists) is remediated by a full debriefing statement at the end of each
survey (drafted below) — standard practice for vignette-experiment literature, not deception under
APA/federal guidelines, and structurally identical in kind to Tariff Paper's own justification.

## Risk/Benefit

Minimal risk — no greater than everyday life. Participants read a brief, realistic vignette about a
city adopting a camera-network policy (or, for the Pretest, answer wording-comprehension items) and
respond to standard attitude-perception items. No physical, legal, financial, or lasting
psychological risk. **One risk worth naming plainly, more than Tariff Paper's package needed to:**
this study's topic (police, surveillance, federal immigration data-sharing) is more likely than a
corporate-pricing topic to touch personally salient or emotionally charged material for some
respondents — e.g., a respondent with direct experience of ICE enforcement action, a wrongful stop,
or general distrust of police. The vignette and items are written at the level of general civic/
policy attitudes (not asking about a respondent's own encounters with law enforcement), which
substantially limits this risk, but it is not zero the way a hypothetical retail price increase is.
Recommend the consent language name this plainly rather than only describing "mild, transient
annoyance," and consider including a brief note on where to find support resources if the topic
raises concern (standard practice in survey research touching policing/immigration topics, not
required by minimal-risk exemption but a reasonable added protection given the subject matter).
Benefits are societal/scientific: empirical evidence on what disclosure practices predict public
trust and opposition regarding surveillance technology, informative for both academic theory and
local-government transparency policy. Risk/benefit ratio favorable, though the risk side is not
identical to Tariff Paper's and shouldn't be described in identical language.

## Debriefing statement (Study 2 and Pretest) — first draft

We want to tell you a bit more about what this research is really about. This study examines how
the *way* a city discloses — or fails to disclose — who can access data from an automated
license-plate-reader (ALPR) camera network affects how fair, trustworthy, and worth opposing or
supporting that camera network people find it. The city and camera-network description you read
was one of two different versions used in this study, describing a realistic but not actual
policy scenario, written for research purposes based on real, publicly reported cases (not any
