# Vignette Realism Notes — Real Consumer-Facing Phrasing Patterns

Purpose: describe the *register and structure* of how real companies talk to
consumers about tariffs, to help Study 2's vignette wording feel authentic.
Not a recommendation to copy any real company's exact language verbatim —
that's a copyright/realism-vs-artifice tradeoff for Britton to weigh, not
this pass's call.

## Observed patterns

1. **Explicit attribution is often paired with a personal/values framing,
   not just a factual one.** Dame's CEO didn't just state a fact ("prices
   are higher because of tariffs") — she framed it as a transparency value
   ("I want customers to understand why it's more expensive—that it's
   because of political decisions"). Real consumer-facing messaging leans on
   *why we're telling you this* as much as *what caused it*. A vignette that
   only states the causal fact without any relational/values framing may
   read as less realistic than one that includes a line like "we want to be
   upfront with you about why."

2. **Legal/technical precision (naming specific tariff sections, HTS codes,
   exact percentages) shows up in B2B-adjacent or more technical consumer
   contexts (KDAR/Hot Max, Honeywell), but general-consumer DTC brands
   (Dame, Labucq) use looser, more colloquial causal language** ("political
   decisions," "remain sustainable") rather than citing statutes. For a
   mainstream retail-consumer vignette (which is what Study 2's design
   targets — Nike/Mattel/Williams-Sonoma-style framing per the design-lock
   note), the looser/colloquial register is the more realistic match; overly
   legal-sounding attribution language (e.g., naming "Section 301") may
   read as more authentic for an industrial/B2B vignette than a mainstream
   retail one.

3. **Quantified partial-absorption claims read as concrete and specific
   dollar/percentage figures, not vague reassurance.** Dame's "$5 doesn't
   cover all of it" and the locked corpus's Birkenstock/Lovesac examples
   share this trait — mitigation-effort narratives that included a number
   felt more credible/specific than a general statement like "we're trying
   to keep costs down." Worth keeping the shared-burden-absorption vignette
   cell quantified (a specific figure or percentage) rather than a vague
   restraint statement, to match how real companies actually phrase it.

4. **Full pass-through announcements in this sample skipped elaborate
   justification and were comparatively brief** (Labucq's two social-media
   price-increase posts, Honeywell's surcharge announcement) — the
   explanation was present but not extensively hedged or apologetic,
   compared to the more hedging, restraint-signaling language of the
   partial-absorption cases (KDAR, Dame). This is a useful register cue:
   the full-pass-through vignette cell can be shorter and more matter-of-
   fact; the absorption cell can include more relational/reassuring
   language, without needing to invent tone from scratch.

5. **The most dramatic real-world case (Amazon Haul) was about *disclosure
   format* itself, not wording** — whether to show a surcharge line at all,
   not how to phrase one. This is a reminder that the Attribution Frame
   manipulation in Study 2 is really manipulating a real, live, politically
   charged corporate decision (whether/how to disclose), not a hypothetical
   one — useful context for the Introduction's motivation, separate from
   vignette wording itself.
