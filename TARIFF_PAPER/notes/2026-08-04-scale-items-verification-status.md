# 2026-08-04 — Scale item wording: what's verified vs. what isn't

Ran multiple targeted searches (and one direct PDF fetch attempt, which failed — the Xia et
al. PDF came back as unparseable binary) to pull verbatim item wording for the five scales in
the lean model. Result: **one scale came back fully verified, four did not.** Being explicit
about this rather than presenting reconstructed wording as confirmed — a wrong item in an
actual IRB submission or published instrument is a real problem, not a formatting detail.

## VERIFIED (via search, cross-confirmed across multiple sources)

**Trust in the Company — Chaudhuri & Holbrook (2001), *Journal of Marketing*, 65(2), 81-93.**
1. I trust this brand.
2. I rely on this brand.
3. This is an honest brand.
4. This brand is safe.

7-point Likert (strongly disagree–strongly agree), original α = .81. Substitute "this brand"
→ "Meridian Home" or "this company" for the tariff context.

## NOT VERIFIED — reconstructed from general knowledge, needs your direct check against the original PDF

**Purchase Intention — Dodds, Monroe & Grewal (1991), *JMR*, 28(3), 307-319.**
Fairly confident on this one — it's reproduced almost verbatim across an unusually large
number of citing papers, more so than the others below:
1. The likelihood of purchasing this product is high.
2. If I were going to buy a product like this, I would consider buying this one.
3. The probability that I would consider buying this product is high.

7-point Likert. **Confidence: moderate-high**, but still unverified this session — pull the
original before locking the instrument.

**Perceived Price Fairness — Xia, Monroe & Cox (2004), *JM*, 68, 1-15.**
Commonly-reproduced core items, typically semantic-differential:
1. This price increase is [unfair / fair].
2. This price increase is [unacceptable / acceptable].
3. This price increase is [unreasonable / reasonable].

**Confidence: moderate.** Some versions of this scale add a 4th item; word order/exact
adjective pairs need checking against the original.

**Word-of-Mouth Intention — Maxham & Netemeyer (2002), *JM*, 66(4), 57-71.**
Likely close to this pattern (this construct's wording is closely shared across Maxham &
Netemeyer and the earlier Zeithaml, Berry & Parasuraman 1996 battery it draws on, which makes
we less sure whose exact wording is whose):
1. I would say positive things about [Company] to other people.
2. I would recommend [Company] to someone who seeks my advice.
3. I would encourage friends and relatives to do business with [Company].

**Confidence: moderate-low** — worth explicitly checking whether Maxham & Netemeyer's actual
wording differs from the Zeithaml et al. version before deciding which to cite.

**Perceived Opportunism / Inferred Motive — Campbell (1999), *JMR*, 36(2), 187-199 or 187-200
(sources disagree by one page — verify the exact range when you pull it; a 2009 *Journal of
Business Research* reference list gives 187-200, most other sources give 187-199).**
This is the **least confident reconstruction of the five** — despite a second round of
targeted searches (including two direct PDF fetch attempts, both blocked by binary/encoded
PDF streams neither WebFetch nor the Read tool's PDF parser could get past on these specific
files), I still don't have Campbell's exact item wording. One partial lead worth noting: one
search summary described "a three-item measure of price fairness perceptions using a 1-7
scale with the items 'unfair/fair,' 'unreasonable/reasonable,' and 'wrong/right'" in
connection with this paper — but I can't confirm whether that's Campbell's actual *fairness
outcome* measure (plausible) or got conflated with a different paper by the search summary,
and either way it's the fairness DV, not the inferred-motive item this design actually needs.
Illustrative-only placeholders, not to be used as-is:
1. I think the seller raised the price to take advantage of the situation. (illustrative only)
2. I think the seller raised the price to increase its profit margin. (illustrative only)
3. (reverse-coded) I think the seller had a legitimate reason for raising the price. (illustrative only)

**Do not use these three as-is.** This is the most load-bearing scale in the whole design
(it's the construct tied directly to the Attribution Frame manipulation and Study 1's Theme
2) and the one I'm least sure about — it needs your direct pull from the original JMR article
before anything else on this list. I've hit the ceiling of what's findable through search and
web-fetchable PDFs; this one genuinely needs your library access.
