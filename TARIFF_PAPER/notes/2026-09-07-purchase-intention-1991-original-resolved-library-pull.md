## The actual Appendix — read directly, full verbatim text

Dodds, Monroe & Grewal (1991) do **not** use the label "Purchase Intention" —
the construct is called **"Willingness to Buy"** throughout, operationalized as a
**5-item, 7-point scale** (Appendix, p. 318):

1. "The likelihood of purchasing this product is:" (very high to very low)
2. "If I were going to buy this product, I would consider buying this model at the
   price shown" (strongly agree to strongly disagree)
3. "At the price shown, I would consider buying the product" (strongly agree to
   strongly disagree)
4. "The probability that I would consider buying the product is:" (very high to
   very low)
5. "My willingness to buy the product is:" (very high to very low)

Reported reliability in the original: coefficient alpha .97 (calculator) and .96
(stereo headset player), average interitem correlations .85 and .83 — very strong.

## Recommendation for this study specifically

This project's vignettes manipulate a **tariff-driven price increase presented to
subjects** (per `Introduction_and_Theory_DRAFT_2026-08-12.md`'s H1a framing) — so,
unlike a generic willingness-to-buy context, the original scale's **price-referencing
items (2 and 3) are a strong substantive fit, not a mismatch to avoid.** Subjects in
this design *do* see a specific price, making "at the price shown, I would consider
buying the product" directly usable as written.

**Recommended default: adopt all 5 original items verbatim** (genericizing only
"this model" → "this product" for consistency, a cosmetic change, not a wording
substitution) rather than a truncated 3-item subset — the full scale is the one
with reported .96-.97 reliability, and using it in full is more defensible in a
methods section than any subset chosen after the fact. If the instrument needs to
stay short, items 1, 4, and 5 are the safest 3-item fallback (fully generic, no
