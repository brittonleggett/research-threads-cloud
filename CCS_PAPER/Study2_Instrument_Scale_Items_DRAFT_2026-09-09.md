## 1. Institutional trust (competence-based / integrity-based)

**Terwel, Harinck, Ellemers & Daamen (2009), Risk Analysis, 29(8), 1129-1140** — the model's
named source for this construct — remains **inaccessible** after a real attempt tonight (Wiley
403, no open-access mirror found). Its exact items could not be pulled. Needs a library pull if
its precise wording specifically is wanted.

**Real, verified alternative — full text obtained**: **Liu, L., Bouman, T., Perlaviciute, G., &
Steg, L. (2020). "Effects of competence- and integrity-based trust on public acceptability of
renewable energy projects in China and the Netherlands." Journal of Environmental Psychology, 67,
101390.** This paper operationalizes the *same* competence-based/integrity-based trust
distinction Terwel et al. (2009) originated (explicitly cites and builds on it), with real,
verbatim manipulation-check items, 7-point semantic-differential scale (-3 to +3):

*Competence-based trust* (2 items, mean/average):
- "has little experience in developing [X] projects" — "has much experience in developing [X]
  projects"
- "has little knowledge in developing [X] projects" — "has extensive knowledge in developing [X]
  projects"

*Integrity-based trust* (3 items, mean/average):
- "is dishonest about its activities" — "is honest about its activities"
- "is not transparent about its activities" — "is transparent about its activities"
- "took interests of local residents very little into account in the past" — "took interests of
  local residents very much into account in the past"

Adapt "[X]" to "CCS" and the referent from "the energy company" to "state regulators" (matching
the paper's own governance-frame focus, not a private company). **Cite as adapted from Liu et al.
(2020), itself building on Terwel et al. (2009)'s original construct** — do not cite as if these
are Terwel et al.'s own verbatim items, since they are not; this is an honest, real, transparent
adaptation chain, not the original source's exact wording.

**Simpler alternative single item, real and verified**, from Firestone, Hirt, Bidwell, Gardner &
Dwyer (2020), ERSS 62, 101393 (Block Island offshore wind study, full text obtained): "[Actor]
acted in an open and trustworthy manner" — 5-category agree/disagree scale. Plainer, more
readable than the semantic-differential items above, and from a US energy-infrastructure-siting
study rather than a wind-in-China/Netherlands one — may fit this paper's Louisiana context and
readability goals better as a standalone check.

## 2. Procedural justice / voice

**Tyler (2003)** itself is largely a theoretical synthesis piece, not a standalone instrument
paper — no discrete four-item voice/neutrality/trustworthiness/dignity battery was found
attributable to Tyler (2003) directly tonight. **Sunshine & Tyler (2003), Law & Society Review,
37, 513-547** — the paper the model already leans on for procedural-justice theory — was not
reachable at item-level tonight (search-snippet only, confirms the theoretical framing but not
exact item text).

**Real, verified alternative, full text obtained**: Firestone et al. (2020) again — real,
verbatim "voice" items, 5-category agree/disagree scale:
- "Community members had a say in the development of [the relevant plan/process]"
- "Community members had a say in the planning process of [the project]"

And a composite **process-fairness** item set (5-category, "very unfair" to "very fair"):
- "[Belief that] the [OSAMP-equivalent/overall plan] process was fair"
- "[Belief that] the state permitting process was fair"

These are plain-language, directly adaptable to CCS's governance-frame conditions (e.g., "State
regulators gave residents a say in how the well was permitted"), and come from a real US
energy-siting study rather than a theoretical synthesis paper — recommend using these as the
paper's actual procedural-justice/voice items, citing Firestone et al. (2020) directly, rather
than trying to reverse-engineer unverified items from Tyler/Sunshine & Tyler.

## 3. Perceived legitimacy

**Gehman, Lefsrud & Fast (2017), Canadian Public Administration, 60(2), 293-317** — confirmed
tonight (via search, not full fetch) to be a conceptual/model-testing paper (tests a model
relating impacts on social infrastructure, contact quality, and procedural fairness to trust,
which in turn relates to acceptance/legitimacy) rather than a paper offering a discrete,
citable legitimacy-perception item battery. **No clean survey instrument was found under this
citation** — flag this honestly rather than inventing items and attributing them to Gehman et al.

**RESOLVED (2026-09-09) — real, validated, high-reliability legitimacy scale found and full-text
verified.** Pan, Yakhmi, Iyer, Strasnick, Zhang & Bernstein (2022), "Comparing the Perceived
Legitimacy of Content Moderation Processes: Contractors, Algorithms, Expert Panels, and Digital
Juries," *Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact.*, 6(CSCW1), Article 82. Not an energy/CCS paper (it's
about social-media content moderation), but it operationalizes "perceived institutional
legitimacy" as a real, five-item, empirically validated scale (Cronbach's α = 0.92 in their data),
explicitly adapted from the political-science tradition of measuring institutional legitimacy —
Gibson, Caldeira & Baird (1998), "On the Legitimacy of National High Courts," *American Political
Science Review*, 92, and Gibson & Caldeira (2003), "Defenders of democracy? Legitimacy, popular
acceptance, and the South African Constitutional Court," *Journal of Politics*, 65(1) — the same
Gibson tradition the model's own theory section already treats as a serious alternative to Tyler.
5-point Likert scale, real verbatim items (Pan et al.'s Table 1):

1. **Outcome Satisfaction**: "I am satisfied with the way [the actor] handled this decision."
2. **Trustworthiness**: "[The actor] can be trusted."
3. **Fairness and Impartiality**: "[The actor] can be fair and impartial."
4. **Institutional Commitment**: "[The state] should keep using [this approach] to make [these]
   decisions."
5. **Decisional Jurisdiction**: "[The actor] should be the authority making [these] decisions."

Adapt "[the actor]"/"[this approach]" to CCS's governance-frame conditions (e.g., for the
Tribal-consultation condition: "State regulators should keep using tribal consultation to make
permitting decisions," "Tribal consultation should be part of the authority making permitting
decisions"). **Cite as adapted from Pan et al. (2022), itself building on Gibson's institutional-
legitimacy tradition** — an honest, transparent adaptation chain across domains (content
moderation → CCS governance), not a claim that these are energy-specific validated items. This is
a genuinely different, better-fitting solution than trying to force Gehman et al.'s conceptual
paper into an instrument it doesn't contain.

**Recommended alternative if Britton prefers not to cross-domain-adapt**: treat legitimacy as
measured via the **support/opposition DV itself** (Section 4 below) plus the process-fairness
composite (Section 2) as its behavioral/attitudinal manifestation — consistent with how Firestone
et al. (2020) and the Liu et al. (2020) mediation chain both operationalize "acceptability" as the
downstream outcome of trust/fairness, without a separate intervening "legitimacy" item battery.

## 4. Support / opposition (DV)

**Primary candidate, already fully verified earlier tonight**: Anders, Liebe & Meyerhoff (2024,
Nature Climate Change) — the design's own closest methodological precedent — used two 11-point
(-5 to +5) items: "How acceptable is this CCS development scenario to you?" and "How fair is the
proposed storage of CO2 ... to you?" Real, exact, already confirmed via full-text read.

**Alternative, simpler, real and verified**: Firestone et al. (2020)'s 5-category support item
("supports" to "opposes" the project) — plainer language, matches this paper's other adopted
items from the same source, may aid readability/consistency if used alongside Section 1-2's
Firestone-sourced items.

## Recommendation

Given tonight's finds, **the cleanest, most internally consistent instrument would combine
Firestone et al. (2020)'s real, plain-language, US-energy-siting items for trust/voice/fairness/
support**, supplemented by Anders et al. (2024)'s two-item acceptability/fairness measure as the
primary DV (since it's the design's own precedent), and **Pan et al. (2022)'s five-item, α=.92
legitimacy scale (adapted from Gibson's institutional-legitimacy tradition)** as the now-resolved
legitimacy mediator. The Liu et al. (2020) competence/integrity split remains available as a
richer trust measure if Britton wants that finer distinction instead of Firestone's single trust
item. **All four mediator/moderator/DV constructs in the conceptual model now have real, sourced
item-level wording** — the instrument-building gap flagged in the conceptual model doc is closed
as of this update, pending Britton's review of the specific cross-domain adaptations made.

## 5. Moderators — status (gap closed this morning, 2026-09-10)

The conceptual model doc (`Conceptual_Model_and_Theory_v2_DRAFT_2026-09-08.md`, Section 7) names
nine moderators, none of which had sourced item wording anywhere in this file as of last night —
this section had been entirely skipped in favor of the mediator/DV work. Pressure-testing the
instrument against the model surfaced this as the real remaining gap, not anything in the four
constructs above. Splitting the nine by what they actually need:

**No scale needed — standard single-item/categorical convention, not a citation question:**
- *Landowner status* — factual, e.g. "Do you own the land you currently live on? Yes/No" (+ own
  mineral rights, separately, if relevant to the LA sample).
- *Proximity* — factual, ZIP code or self-reported distance to nearest active/proposed CCS
  injection site; geocode against the corpus's known project sites rather than trusting
  self-report alone if feasible.
- *Prior O&G employment* — factual/categorical, "Have you or an immediate family member ever
  worked in the oil & gas industry? Yes/No," standard in Gulf Coast energy-attitude surveys.
- *Political ideology* — standard 7-point liberal-conservative self-placement single item; this is
  a methodological convention (used across the energy-siting literature broadly, including
  Firestone et al. 2020's own demographic battery), not something requiring its own citation.

**Climate concern — RESOLVED at the full-scale level (2026-09-10), short-form still open.**
Pulled the actual verbatim 15-item **Dunlap, Van Liere, Mertig & Jones (2000)** New Ecological
Paradigm scale in full via a real open-access secondary source: Wells & Petherick (2016),
*"New Ecological Paradigm and Sustainability Attitudes with Respect to a Multi-Cultural
Educational Milieu in China,"* International Conferences ITS/ICEduTech/STE 2016, Table 1 —
itself a direct, properly-cited reproduction of Dunlap et al.'s own Table 1, not a paraphrase.
Real, verbatim, 5-point-Likert items (7 are reverse-scored, marked *R*):

1. We are approaching the limit of the number of people the earth can support.
2. Humans have the right to modify the natural environment to suit their needs. *(R)*
3. When humans interfere with nature it often produces disastrous consequences.
4. Human ingenuity will ensure that we do not make the earth unliveable. *(R)*
5. Humans are severely abusing the environment.
6. The earth has plenty of natural resources if we just learn how to develop them. *(R)*
7. Plants and animals have as much right as humans to exist.
8. The balance of nature is strong enough to cope with the impacts of modern industrial nations. *(R)*
9. Despite their special abilities humans are still subject to the laws of nature.
10. The so-called "ecological crisis" facing humankind has been greatly exaggerated. *(R)*
11. The earth is like a spaceship with very limited room and resources.
12. Humans are meant to rule over the rest of nature. *(R)*
13. The balance of nature is very delicate and easily upset.
14. Humans will eventually learn enough about how nature works to be able to control it. *(R)*
15. If things continue on their present course we will soon experience a major ecological catastrophe.

**The 2025 5-item efficient short-form's specific retained items remain unverified** — ScienceDirect,

scale now, since it's real and fully verified, or (b) holding for a future session with library

**Perceived benefits/risks — still genuinely inaccessible, honestly flagged, not fabricated.**
**Xu, Liu, Chen & Lu (2023), Journal of Environmental Management** (PMID 37801951) remains real and
on-topic (confirmed again via PubMed and Semantic Scholar's own API tonight, both closed-access) but
its item wording could not be pulled through any open channel tried tonight: ScienceDirect (403),
ResearchGate (403), OUCI mirror (metadata only, no items), Semantic Scholar API (abstract only,
`openAccessPdf` field empty/closed). **This remains the single highest-value library pull for a
future session** — still the only CCS-specific validated risk/benefit scale found in this project's
entire lit review, better than any cross-domain adaptation.
- *CCS familiarity* — no single dominant validated scale found, but the convention across this
  literature (confirmed via this morning's search of the CCS-public-perception review literature)
  is a simple single item: self-rated familiarity (e.g., 1-5 "never heard of it" to "very
  familiar") sometimes paired with a factual awareness check ("had you heard of CCS before today,
  yes/no"). Anders et al. (2024)'s own design uses a mandatory pre-vignette information screen
  rather than measuring baseline familiarity as a moderator — worth deciding whether this paper
  wants familiarity as a measured moderator, an equalized-via-information-screen design element
  (Anders et al.'s approach), or both.
- *Baseline (pre-vignette) trust* — likely the simplest resolution is administering the same
  Section 1 trust items (Liu et al. 2020 or Firestone et al. 2020 single-item) pre-vignette, asked
  about "state regulators generally" rather than the specific CCS project — i.e., reuse, not a new
  scale. Britton's call whether a pre/post design is worth the added survey length.

**Rural identity — RESOLVED, real verbatim items found (2026-09-10).** The candidate flagged
earlier (Oser et al. 2022, *Journal of Rural Health*, "The Rural Identity Scale," 15 items,
α=.72-.83) is real and validated but sits behind a Wiley paywall — item wording not pulled. A
**shorter, real, verbatim, open-access alternative was found instead**: Krok-Schoen et al. (2015),
*"The Conceptualization of Self-Identity among Residents of Appalachia, Ohio,"* quoted directly
(with citation) in an open-access PMC paper — Justice et al., *"Rural-specific identity and
associations with lifestyle behaviors and well-being among rural cancer survivors,"* PMC13370668 —
which is itself an honest secondary-source chain, same pattern as this file's other adapted items,
not a claim of having read Krok-Schoen (2015) directly. Real 4-item, 4-point-Likert battery:

1. "Your general attitudes and opinions are similar to people who live in rural communities."
2. "You are typical of people who live in rural communities."
3. "You identify with people who live in rural communities."
4. "You see yourself belonging to a rural community."

Adapt "rural communities" to name the specific Louisiana parish/region context where relevant.
**Recommend this 4-item version over pursuing Oser et al.'s 15-item RIS** — it's real, verbatim,
already confirmed via legitimate open-access reproduction, and vastly shorter, which matters given
how long this moderator block already is. Cite as Krok-Schoen et al. (2015), noting it was sourced
via Justice et al.'s open-access reproduction rather than the original if a reviewer asks for the
chain of custody.
