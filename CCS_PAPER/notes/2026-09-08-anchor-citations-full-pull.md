# 2026-09-08 — Full-text pull of the lit review's six "most contemporary anchor" citations

Follow-up to `2026-09-08-lit-review-foundation-digest.md`'s own suggested next step. Per
Britton's explicit "always pull lit" instruction, this pass also backward-snowballed for
additional strong citations beyond the six originally named.

## Results by citation

**1. Alexander & Stanley (2022)** — still snippet-level only (SAGE, ResearchGate, Academia.edu
all 403'd), but confirmed via multiple independent search excerpts, not a single source. Core
argument: CCS functions as a "colonial flanking mechanism" — its legitimation function can't be
understood without settler-colonial analysis, because it reproduces settler entitlements to
Indigenous lands/bodies/ecosystems by governing the *appearance* of harm-mitigation rather than
the underlying extractive relation. **Direct design implication:** the Tribal-consultation
vignette condition isn't just one neutral option among six governance frames — Alexander &
Stanley's own argument implies that condition itself risks *becoming* the flanking-mechanism
dynamic they critique if handled carelessly. Reinforces why the lit review's Part H ethics
guardrails aren't just CYA — they're substantively tied to the theory itself.

**2. Stephanides et al. (2025) — FULL TEXT obtained** (Cronfa/Swansea open-access repository).
Confirms the four-perspective framework (acceptance / acceptability / situated responsiveness /
systemic responsiveness) with a full comparison table across intention, sociotechnical object,
view of the public, temporality, and governance implications. Based on a PRISMA-style scoping
review of 500+ documented UK public-engagement cases across wind, GGR, and smart-homes. **Two
real findings worth building in:** (a) the four perspectives **co-exist and compete** rather than
progress sequentially — meaning a single governance-frame vignette condition is implicitly
testing which "logic" a given respondent defaults to, not just measuring one fixed construct; (b)
their own CCUS case section found real UK CCUS Public Dialogue evaluations documented
"underrepresentation of expert stakeholders opposing CCUS" and processes run as "tick-box
exercises" — **directly citable evidence of institutional-legitimacy-manipulation/tokenistic
consultation in the CCS literature itself**, giving the reframed ERSS design a real way to keep
a version of Track C's original manufactured-legitimacy angle, just grounded in this literature
instead of the astroturfing/push-polling literature.

**3. Anders, Liebe & Meyerhoff (2024)** — not reached at full-text level (paywalled, no
open-access mirror found this pass). Still only the lit-review digest's summary: five-country
multifactorial vignette experiment, fairness perceptions (not compensation) drive CCS acceptance
for cross-border CO2 transport specifically. **This is the paper's own primary methodological
template and its exact vignette wording is still not in hand** — worth a dedicated follow-up pull
(Ole Miss library/Consensus.app) before the Study 2 instrument gets built, not just a nice-to-have.

**4. San Román-Niaves, Morandini & Pietrantoni (2026)** — real detail beyond the digest's one-liner
confirmed. SEM, N=800 (Poland, Czechia). Refined finding: trust boosts perceived benefits AND
relates positively to perceived risk — both mediate trust→acceptance, with perceived benefit the
strongest single predictor. **Non-obvious, useful finding: higher trust increases risk
sensitivity without reducing support** — trust doesn't numb people to risk, it can coexist with
clear-eyed risk awareness while still sustaining support. Worth building this nuance into the
institutional-trust mediator's hypothesized relationships rather than assuming trust simply
suppresses risk perception.

**5. Minadakis & Vega-Araújo (2024)** — abstract-level only (ScienceDirect, SEI both blocked).
Confirmed: argues SLO (extractive-industry origin) and Social Acceptance (renewables literature)
converge despite different vocabularies, with energy justice proposed as the tool that makes SLO
empirically measurable rather than just rhetorical. Directly supports the lit review's own SLO
framing move.

**6. Ó Maonaigh, Fitzgerald & Reilly (2025)** — abstract-level only. Confirmed intersectional
critique: SLO is typically treated as a coalition-building tool between developers and
"communities," obscuring real inequalities in who has power to support or resist projects.
**Design implication: reframe the moderator list (landowner status, rural identity) through an
intersectional "who has standing to be consulted" lens rather than as independent demographic
controls** — a real refinement to Part F3's moderator table, not just a citation to add.

## New citations found via backward snowball (not in the original lit review)

- **Robb, Le Billon & Bakker (2024), "Landscapes of Recarbonization: Carbon Neutrality, Settler
  Colonialism, and Cumulative Environmental Effects in the Peace River Region, Canada," *Annals
  of the American Association of Geographers*, 114(9), 1930-1947.** Confirmed via abstract: a
  strong, recent companion to Alexander & Stanley — examines net-zero decarbonization as a
  spatial process with ongoing colonial-violence effects in a Canadian case region. Worth adding
  alongside Alexander & Stanley as a second settler-colonialism/CCS-adjacent anchor.
- **"When a forest is masked by trees: How French subsurface industries involved in
  decarbonisation and transition policies are instrumentalising poor social acceptance"**
  (ScienceDirect/ERSS) — found by title only, **not independently verified**, flagged as a
  promising lead specifically for the institutional-legitimacy-manipulation angle (the title
  itself suggests industry manufacturing/instrumentalizing acceptance narratives) but needs its
  own real verification pass before citing.
- **"Enabling Agri Energy Justice"** (2026, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) —
  agricultural/wind energy-justice framing, lower priority, possible supporting citation for the
  landowner-status moderator only.
