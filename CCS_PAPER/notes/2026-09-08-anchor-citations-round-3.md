# 2026-09-08 — Round 3: Sovacool et al. (2024) + five more snowballed citations, all verified

## Sovacool, Baum & Fritz (2024), World Development 183, 106719 — abstract/detail level, not full text

Confirmed real (ScienceDirect PII S0305750X2400189X). Massive cross-national survey: n=30,284
across 30 countries/19 languages, on public preferences for 10 climate-intervention technologies
(carbon removal + solar radiation management), examined through minority-group membership,
Indigenousness, and place/rurality. **Genuinely counter-intuitive finding:** minority and
Indigenous respondents report *greater* familiarity with these technologies than non-members, are
*more* supportive of small-scale trials, more positive toward engineered (vs. nature-based)
options, and less supportive of policy restrictions. **Direct implication for the CCS paper's
Discussion:** this is real, large-N, quantitative evidence against assuming Indigenous/minority
publics are monolithically wary of climate-intervention infrastructure — a caution against
assuming the Tribal-consultation condition would only ever read as a threat signal to respondents,
and a citation worth using to avoid overclaiming what the paper's own single-region design can
say about Indigenous attitudes generally.

## Five further snowballed citations, all verified real

1. **Chailleux, Arnauld de Sartre & Briday (2023), "Ecological modernisation wanderings: ambivalent
   framing and unstable coalitions in the development of CCUS in France," *Journal of Environmental
   Policy and Planning*, 25(4).** Same author team as the primary "masked by trees" find — shows
   this is a sustained CCS-specific research program, not a one-off. Secondary supporting citation.
2. **Lefstad, Allesson, Busch & Carton (2024), "Burying problems? Imaginaries of carbon capture and
   storage in Scandinavia," ERSS 113.** A second, independent CCS-specific source for the
   depoliticization thread, alongside Hansson et al. (2022) already in the original May lit review.
3. **Sovacool, Bell, Daggett, Labuski, Lennon, Naylor, Klinger, Leonard & Firestone (2023),
   "Pluralizing energy justice: Incorporating feminist, anti-racist, Indigenous, and postcolonial
   perspectives," ERSS 97, 102996.** A high-authority theoretical citation for broadening the
   paper's energy-justice framing beyond the three/four-tenet model — good Discussion-section
   citation on the limits of the core conceptual model.
4. **Mejía-Montero, Jenkins, van der Horst & Lane (2023), "An intersectional approach to energy
   justice: individual and collective concerns around wind power on Zapotec land," ERSS 98.** Real
   empirical mixed-methods study on Indigenous land in Mexico, contrasting "pro" landowner and
   "anti" communal-land-holder energy-justice constructs — a genuine methodological precedent for
   treating Indigenous/local perspectives as heterogeneous rather than monolithic.
5. **Hazrati & Heffron (2021), "Conceptualising restorative justice in the energy transition:
   changing the perspectives of fossil fuels," ERSS 78, 102115.** The actual originating paper
   behind the restorative-justice fourth tenet Minadakis & Vega-Araújo (2024) cited secondhand —
   **recommend citing this directly as the primary source** for that construct rather than relying
   on the secondary treatment.

## Sixth, found and independently verified this pass (not just flagged)

**Lefstad & Rubiano (2025), "From the Rainforest to the Fjords: examining equity in carbon capture
and storage climate policy," *Climate Policy*, 25(9), 1368-1382.** DOI 10.1080/14693062.2025.2451644.
Comparative national-policy case study (Brazil and Norway, two leading CCS-development nations)
examining how each frames CCS policy with respect to equity. Finding: current CCS policy design in
both countries risks unjust distributional outcomes and fossil-fuel lock-in; argues CCS's non-
fossil applications (e.g., direct air capture, industrial decarbonization beyond oil/gas) could
improve both its climate and equity profile if policy design deliberately supported them over
fossil-linked uses. Same first author (Lefstad) as item #2 above — a second confirmation she's a
real, active scholar specifically on CCS equity, worth knowing as a name to follow for future
citations (per the standing snowballing method's "follow prolific authors" tip). Not
Louisiana/US-specific — a national-policy-level comparison — but directly on-point for framing the
Introduction's equity argument at the policy-design level, distinct from the paper's own
governance-frame public-perception focus.
