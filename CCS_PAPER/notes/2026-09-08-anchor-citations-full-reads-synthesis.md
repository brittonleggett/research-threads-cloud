# 2026-09-08 — Full reads of all four library-pulled papers: synthesis and design implications

All four papers Britton pulled via Ole Miss library access were read in full (not just abstracts).
Saved to `Literature/`. This supersedes the abstract-level characterizations in
`2026-09-08-anchor-citations-full-pull.md` and `2026-09-08-anchor-citations-round-2.md`.

## 1. Alexander & Stanley (2022) — even more consequential than the abstract suggested

Not a survey study — a critical essay grounded in real documentary evidence, including FOIA'd
RCMP intelligence records (the "Aboriginal Joint Intelligence Group," AJIG). Core finding: CCS
functions as a "colonial flanking mechanism" — it narrows the legal/technical definition of
"harm" to manageable per-barrel emissions, rendering invisible the actual disproportionate toxic
exposure Indigenous communities face, while the same governance apparatus that promotes CCS has
run parallel, real, surveillance operations against First Nations **specifically for asserting
that consultation was inadequate** — e.g., Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation was surveilled in
2007 for suing Alberta over a "failure to fulfil its duty to consult" before granting tar-sands
extraction permits; several Manitoba First Nations were surveilled for a similar failure-to-
consult federal court appeal against pipelines linked to the tar sands.

**Direct design implication:** the Tribal-consultation vignette condition cannot be framed as a
simple positive governance signal without real risk of naivety. The real-world record this paper
documents is that consultation *disputes* — not just consultation's absence — have historically
been sites of state surveillance and delegitimization of Indigenous jurisdiction. This doesn't
mean dropping the condition; it means the Discussion section needs to engage this directly, and
it strengthens (doesn't just decorate) the existing Part H guardrail that the vignette measures
public perception of the *idea* of consultation, not its actual adequacy or enactment.

## 2. Minadakis & Vega-Araújo (2024) — confirms and refines the SLO/EJ model, adds a construct

Semi-systematic review (1,314 screened, 101 selected). Confirms the four-component SLO model
already in the conceptual digest (fairness & trust, economic & social legitimacy, norms
compliance, credibility) mapped onto the four EJ tenets — but the mapping adds **restorative
justice** as a distinct fourth tenet (alongside distributive/procedural/recognition), tied
specifically to credibility via legally binding agreements, impact assessments, and compensation
commitments. The original lit-review digest's model only had three EJ tenets (distributive,
procedural, recognition) — restorative justice is a real addition worth considering for the
conceptual model, operationalized as e.g. perceived credibility of compensation/restoration
commitments. Also gives the citable term for the anti-pattern: "Decide-Announce-Defend" (DAD),
sourced to Komendantova & Battaglini (2016).

## 3. Ó Maonaigh, Fitzgerald & Reilly (2025) — real empirical study, and two strong new citations

Genuine empirical qualitative study: 34 respondents (16 senior Irish energy-sector leaders, 5
representatives of marginalized groups, 3 rural community focus groups) on Ireland's wind
transition. Core finding: orthodox SLO practice treats communities as homogeneous, missing real
barriers (time poverty, childcare, transport, literacy) that keep marginalized groups out of
consultation even when a formal process exists — directly relevant to the paper's moderator list
(don't just measure landowner status/rural identity as demographic controls; frame them as
"who actually has standing to participate").

**Two strong new citations surfaced via its own reference list, both real and on-point:**
- **Lee, Schelly, Gagnon, Smith & Tiwari (2023), ERSS 97** — "Preferences and perceived barriers
  to pursuing energy sovereignty and renewable energy: a tribal nations perspective." A real
  empirical study centering actual Tribal nations' own views on energy sovereignty — exactly the
  kind of source Part H says the CCS paper itself cannot substitute for, but can and should cite
  to show that literature engaging Indigenous perspectives directly does exist.
- **Sovacool, Baum & Fritz (2024), World Development 183** — "Minority groups, indigenousness and
  indigeneity, and place in social perceptions of future climate interventions." Title alone
  suggests direct relevance to the paper's core question; not yet read in full, worth a follow-up
  pull.

## 4. Arnauld de Sartre & Chailleux (2026, not 2025 as earlier noted — online Jan 2026, ERSS 132,
104517) — the best single find of the day

Real, empirical, full-text-confirmed, peer-reviewed, **published in the paper's own target
journal**, and CCS-specific. Method: 15 interviews specifically on French carbon storage
(2017-2018) plus a corpus of 579 CCS-related French press articles (2002-2017), compared against
parallel analyses of shale gas and mining. Finding: "lack of social acceptance" functions as a
constructed narrative that industry/government coalitions use in three distinct ways — (a) during
conflict, to characterize opponents as ignorant/irrational; (b) after conflict, to mask the real
(often economic or political) reasons a project failed; (c) long after conflict, to justify
regulatory "reforms" that serve project sponsors more than the public. Concrete CCS example:
ArcelorMittal's failed ULCOS project blamed local opposition, while the interview evidence shows
the real reason was steel-market economics (the plant was landlocked while surviving competitors
were port-based) — opposition was a convenient after-the-fact excuse, not the actual cause.

**This is the single strongest replacement for the astroturfing/push-polling literature this
session originally reached for.** It is empirically stronger (interviews + large press corpus,
not search-snippet-level), directly CCS-specific rather than adjacent, and sits in ERSS itself —
removing the earlier verification-level concern entirely. **Recommend this become the primary
citation for the institutional-legitimacy-manipulation angle**, with Cho et al. (2011) and Sassan
et al. (2023) (astroturfing, from earlier in the session) retained as supporting citations rather
than primary ones.

## Net effect on the conceptual model

None of this changes the basic governance-frame → procedural/recognition justice → trust →
legitimacy → support structure. It does:
1. Add restorative justice as a fourth candidate mediator construct (Minadakis & Vega-Araújo).
2. Give the paper a much stronger, CCS-specific citation for the "manufactured narrative" /
   institutional-accountability angle than anything found earlier in the session (Arnauld de
   Sartre & Chailleux).
3. Require the Discussion section to engage directly with the real historical entanglement of
   Tribal consultation disputes and state surveillance (Alexander & Stanley) — a limitation/scope
   note, not a design blocker.
4. Surface two more real citations for engaging Indigenous perspectives responsibly without
   overclaiming (Lee et al. 2023; Sovacool, Baum & Fritz 2024, latter not yet fully verified).
