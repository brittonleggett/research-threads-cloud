# 2026-09-08 — Round 2 anchor-citation pull: one big new find, four still need library access

## Real find, fully open access, read in full: Murrey, Jackson & Volonterio (2023), "Social
Licence to Operate: The Role of an Idea in the Colonial Logics of Extractivism," *Cosmopolitan
Civil Societies*, 15(1), 1-18. Full text via UTS ePress.

This is a sharp, radical critique of the SLO concept itself — not just the milder critiques
already in the lit review (Owen & Kemp 2013, Boutilier 2021). Core argument: SLO isn't a neutral
academic construct — it was invented in 1997 by a mining-industry executive (Jim Cooney, Placer
Dome) as corporate risk management, then legitimized through academic research built on
corporate-aligned sources. Four specific problems the authors raise: (1) SLO research has
corporate-aligned origins; (2) SLO assumes all communities want extraction-based development,
erasing other worldviews; (3) SLO's "weak governance" framing lets corporations self-regulate
instead of following formal consent procedures; (4) SLO presumes corporate self-policing works,
which the authors call demonstrably false. **The sharpest point for this paper specifically: SLO's
vague "acceptance" standard substitutes for Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) — removing the
formal veto power FPIC protocols would give Indigenous communities**, and the authors argue
academic researchers who study/legitimize SLO are themselves complicit in this.

**Why this matters for the CCS design, not just as a citation to add:** the existing lit review's
Part H already recommended positioning the paper as testing *whether* SLO can be redefined through
procedural/recognition justice rather than measuring how much license CCS firms currently enjoy.
Murrey et al. sharpens that same caution considerably — it implies that even a more careful,
justice-reframed SLO study could still be read as reproducing the acceptance-over-consent problem
they describe, *especially* in the Tribal-consultation vignette condition specifically. This is
worth engaging directly in the Discussion/limitations section: explicitly naming that measuring
general-public perception of "Tribal consultation happened" is not, and should never be presented
as, equivalent to actual FPIC — which is exactly what Part H already said, now with a much sharper,
citable theoretical backing for why that distinction matters. This makes the paper's critical
self-awareness stronger and more defensible to ERSS reviewers, not weaker.
