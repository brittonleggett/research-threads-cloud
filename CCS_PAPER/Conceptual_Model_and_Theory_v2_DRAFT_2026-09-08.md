## 1. Core causal model (unchanged from the original design)

**Governance frame (manipulated) → procedural justice perception + recognition justice perception
→ institutional trust → perceived legitimacy → support for CCS infrastructure**

Primary theoretical anchors, unchanged: energy justice triumvirate (McCauley et al. 2013; Jenkins
et al. 2016), procedural justice/legitimacy theory (Tyler 2003), social license to operate (Prno &
Slocombe 2012; Moffat & Zhang 2014). Recognition justice and settler-colonialism critique remain
critical supplements requiring the careful methodological handling in Section 5 below, not
folded into the general public-perception measurement model.

## 2. Change #1 — restorative justice added as a fourth EJ construct

The original May model used a three-tenet EJ framework (distributive, procedural, recognition).
**Add restorative justice as a fourth**, per Minadakis & Vega-Araújo (2024)'s SLO-EJ synergy model
(their Fig. 1 maps it to "credibility," operationalized via legally binding agreements, impact
assessments, and compensation commitments). **Cite the construct's actual originating source
directly: Hazrati & Heffron (2021), ERSS 78, 102115** — not Minadakis & Vega-Araújo's secondary
treatment. Practical effect on the model: consider adding a restorative-justice-perception item
battery (e.g., perceived credibility/adequacy of compensation and remediation commitments in the
vignette) as either a fifth mediator or a component folded into the trust measure — Britton's
call, not resolved here.

## 3. Change #2 — the vignette manipulation itself (already drafted, see
`notes/2026-09-08-original-vignette-draft.md`)

Original May design proposed 6 governance-frame levels (state regulator, industry expertise,
local referendum, property-rights, affected-community consultation, Tribal consultation). **This
session found a real empirical reason to restructure**: Anders, Liebe & Meyerhoff (2024, Nature
Climate Change) — the design's own closest methodological precedent, read in full via Britton's
library pull — found that governance-frame attributes like implementing-body type (industry vs.
public-private vs. government) did **not** significantly affect CCS acceptance across five
countries, while consultation-extent attributes robustly did.

**Result: a redesigned 5-condition manipulation along a pure consultation-extent gradient** (no
consultation → notice-only/tokenistic → affected-community consultation → broader regional
consultation → Tribal consultation), with implementing body held constant across all conditions
(the real Louisiana permitting structure: LDENR with EPA Region 6 oversight) rather than
manipulated. All vignette text is original, written fresh for this study — explicitly not copied
or paraphrased from Anders et al.'s own (unseen) vignette wording, per Britton's standing
priority that nothing in this project risk his academic integrity. Full text and a shared
manipulation-check item already drafted in the file cited above.

**Update 2026-09-09:** the vignette file's v1 text above was superseded same-day by a v2 revision
after real Flesch-Kincaid testing (via `textstat`) found the v1 wording ran FK grade 15-18
("very difficult") — far above the 8th-grade target — driven by bureaucratic vocabulary, not
sentence length. V2 lands at FK 8.2-9.2 for four of five conditions; the Tribal-consultation
condition stays elevated (FK 11.0) by deliberate choice, since simplifying "federally recognized"/
"consultation" further would cost the precision Part H's ethics guardrails require. Use the v2
wording (`notes/2026-09-08-original-vignette-draft.md`, "Readability" section) as the actual
draft going forward.

## 4. Change #3 — the institutional-legitimacy-manipulation angle now has a real primary citation

Early in this session, before the pivot to the May lit review, a search pass tried to ground this
angle in general astroturfing/push-polling literature (Cho et al. 2011; Sassan et al. 2023) —
real but only verified at search-snippet level, and not CCS-specific.

**Replace with a much stronger primary citation, verified in full text**:
**Arnauld de Sartre & Chailleux (2026), "When a forest is masked by trees: How French subsurface
industries involved in decarbonisation and transition policies are instrumentalising poor social
acceptance," ERSS 132, 104517.** Real interview + press-corpus evidence (15 interviews on French
CCS specifically, 579 CCS-related press articles 2002-2017) that "lack of social acceptance" is a
constructed narrative industry/government coalitions use to (a) characterize opponents as
ignorant, (b) mask the real reasons projects fail, (c) justify reforms serving sponsors more than
the public — with a clean CCS example (the failed ULCOS project blaming opposition when the real
cause was steel-market economics). This is CCS-specific, published in ERSS itself, and far more
rigorously verified than the astroturfing literature it replaces. Retain Cho et al. (2011) and
Sassan et al. (2023) as supporting citations, not primary ones. Also add, as secondary supporting
citations from the same author program: Chailleux, Arnauld de Sartre & Briday (2023) and Lefstad,
Allesson, Busch & Carton (2024, a second independent CCS-depoliticization source alongside the
already-cited Hansson et al. 2022).

**Where this fits the model:** not as a new manipulated factor, but as Discussion-section material
interpreting why institutional actors (state regulators, CCS operators) might frame public
reaction the way they do — a citation for the paper's own reflexivity about how "acceptance" gets
constructed and contested, directly usable when discussing the paper's own governance-frame
findings in light of real institutional behavior patterns documented elsewhere.

## 5. Change #4 — the Tribal-consultation condition needs a sharper, better-cited ethics/framing
section than Part H of the original document alone provided

The original lit review's Part H (measure public perception of the idea of consultation, never
claim to speak for Indigenous nations, reach out to Louisiana's federally-recognized tribes if
pursuing this substantively) remains the right operating framework. This session adds real
citation weight and two genuine complications to sit with, not resolve unilaterally:

- **Alexander & Stanley (2022)**, read in full: real FOIA'd RCMP surveillance evidence that
  Indigenous nations have historically been surveilled and delegitimized *specifically for
  disputing that consultation was adequate* (e.g., Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation surveilled in
  2007 for suing over a failure-to-consult claim). **This means the Tribal-consultation condition
  cannot be framed as an unambiguously positive governance signal without addressing, in the
  Discussion, that consultation processes themselves have been real historical sites of contested
  legitimacy and state power** — not just a procedural checkbox.
- **Murrey, Jackson & Volonterio (2023)**, read in full (open access): a sharper critique arguing
  SLO's vague "acceptance" standard substitutes for actual Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC),
  removing the formal veto power FPIC protocols would give. Reinforces that the paper must state
  explicitly and repeatedly that measuring public perception of a Tribal-consultation frame is not,
  and must never be presented as, a proxy for actual Indigenous consent.
- **Counter-caution, from Sovacool, Baum & Fritz (2024)**, a real n=30,284/30-country survey: minority
  and Indigenous respondents in that study were *more*, not less, supportive of climate-intervention
  technologies and small-scale trials than non-Indigenous respondents — a genuine complication
  against assuming the Tribal-consultation condition would read as uniformly negative or
  threat-signaling to general-public respondents. Don't let the critical-theory citations above
  become an assumption that the manipulation's effect direction is obvious; it's an open empirical
  question the vignette experiment itself should actually test.
- **For genuinely engaging Indigenous perspectives as heterogeneous, not monolithic**, if the paper
  wants to go further than the general-public survey design allows on its own: **Lee, Schelly,
  Gagnon, Smith & Tiwari (2023), ERSS 97** (a real empirical study centering actual Tribal nations'
  own energy-sovereignty views) and **Mejía-Montero, Jenkins, van der Horst & Lane (2023), ERSS 98**
  (empirical intersectional energy-justice study contrasting "pro" and "anti" Indigenous
  landholders' differing constructs on Zapotec land) are both real, citable models — not
  replacements for direct Tribal engagement, but evidence such engagement is methodologically
  established and expected in this literature.
- **Sovacool, Bell, Daggett, Labuski, Lennon, Naylor, Klinger, Leonard & Firestone (2023), ERSS 97,
  102996** ("Pluralizing energy justice") is a strong, high-authority citation for a Discussion-
  section paragraph on the limits of even a four-tenet EJ model and the need for
  feminist/anti-racist/Indigenous/postcolonial perspectives beyond it.

## 6. Change #5 — a national-policy-level equity citation for the Introduction

**Lefstad & Rubiano (2025), "From the Rainforest to the Fjords: examining equity in carbon capture
and storage climate policy," Climate Policy, 25(9), 1368-1382.** A comparative Brazil/Norway
CCS-equity policy study — not US/Louisiana-specific, but useful for framing the Introduction's
equity argument at the policy-design level, distinct from this paper's own governance-frame
public-perception focus. Same first author as item in Section 4 (Lefstad) — a real, active
CCS-equity scholar worth tracking for future citations.
