# Claude Code Research Project Kickoff Prompt
## Legalized Sports Gambling, State Revenue, and Social Cost

You are helping launch a new academic research project examining the social and economic consequences of legalized sports gambling in the United States, with particular attention to **mobile/online sports betting**, **state tax revenue**, **consumer welfare**, and **social costs/externalities**.

This is a research-development task, not a paper-writing task. Your job is to build a rigorous, organized foundation for the project so the research team can decide whether the idea has a defensible contribution and, if so, how to execute it.

Do **not** rush to produce hypotheses, write an introduction, or draft a manuscript before completing the literature and feasibility audit below.

---

# 1. Initial Project Idea

The motivating question is:

> **Does the revenue generated by legalized sports betting compensate for the measurable financial and social costs associated with its legalization and expansion?**

The specific phenomenon of interest is the rapid expansion of legal sports gambling following the 2018 repeal of PASPA, especially the subsequent adoption of **mobile and online sports betting**.

The project is motivated by a broader public-policy tension:

- States receive tax revenue from gambling.
- Sportsbooks and related firms aggressively promote gambling.
- Mobile betting dramatically reduces friction and makes gambling continuously accessible.
- Emerging research suggests sports-betting legalization may affect household savings, debt, delinquency, bankruptcy, and other financial outcomes.
- Other possible externalities include gambling-disorder treatment, mental health, suicide, substance use, family instability, crime, public assistance, and other social costs.
- Legalization may also cannibalize lottery revenue, casino revenue, or other existing state gambling revenues.
- At the same time, legalization may generate meaningful fiscal benefits and could replace illegal-market betting.

The project should **not assume** that legalized sports gambling is socially harmful or socially beneficial. Treat the net effect as an empirical question.

# 2. Central Research Question

Begin with this broad question:

> **What is the net public-policy effect of legalized sports betting when state fiscal benefits are evaluated alongside measurable consumer and social costs?**

Potentially useful subsidiary questions include:

1. What financial benefits do states receive from legalized sports betting?
2. Does sports betting create genuinely new state revenue or partially cannibalize lottery, casino, or other gambling revenue?
3. What causal evidence exists that legalization affects:
   - household savings,
   - investment,
   - credit scores,
   - consumer debt,
   - delinquency,
   - bankruptcy,
   - financial distress,
   - gambling disorder,
   - mental health,
   - suicide,
   - alcohol/substance use,
   - domestic or family outcomes,
   - crime,
   - public assistance,
   - healthcare utilization,
   - or other measurable social outcomes?
4. Are effects substantially different when **mobile/online betting** becomes legal compared with retail-only betting?
5. Are particular populations disproportionately affected, such as:
   - young adults,
   - men,
   - low-income households,
   - low-savings households,
   - financially constrained consumers,
   - college students,
   - or residents of economically distressed counties?
6. Do states differ in outcomes according to:
   - tax rates,
   - promotional rules,
   - advertising restrictions,
   - responsible-gambling requirements,
   - sportsbook market concentration,
   - number of operators,
   - legalization structure,
   - or treatment funding?
7. Can credible monetary estimates of social costs be compared with state gambling revenue?

Do not force all of these into one study. Determine which questions are empirically feasible and theoretically coherent.

# 3. Possible Core Contribution

One possible contribution is to move beyond papers that examine only one consequence of legalization and instead ask whether the **public revenue produced by sports betting offsets the external costs created by the market**.

A possible conceptual quantity is:

\[
\text{Net Public Benefit}
=
\text{Fiscal Benefits}
-
\text{Monetized External Costs}
\]

A second possibility is:

\[
\text{Social Cost Ratio}
=
\frac{\text{Estimated External Costs}}
{\text{State Sports-Betting Revenue}}
\]

Interpretation:

- Ratio < 1: measured social costs are lower than gambling revenue.
- Ratio = 1: approximately one dollar of measured social cost accompanies each dollar of revenue.
- Ratio > 1: measured social costs exceed state revenue.

These are **starting concepts only**. Do not use them unless the literature and available data make such calculations defensible.

Distinguish carefully between:

- private losses to gamblers,
- transfers between gamblers and gambling firms,
- tax revenue,
- genuine economic/social externalities,
- public expenditures,
- opportunity costs,
- consumer-welfare losses,
- and costs borne by third parties.

Do **not** classify every dollar lost gambling as a social cost.

# 9. Causal Identification Strategy

Evaluate whether a staggered-adoption natural experiment is viable.

Potential starting design:

- State × year or state × quarter panel.
- Treatment based on sports-betting launch.
- Prefer separate treatment indicators for:
  - retail sports betting,
  - online/mobile sports betting.

Evaluate:

- staggered Difference-in-Differences,
- modern DiD estimators robust to treatment timing,
- event studies,
- synthetic control for selected states,
- border-county designs,
- county-level exposure,
- triple differences,
- or other quasi-experimental strategies.

Do **not** automatically use a two-way fixed-effects estimator without checking whether staggered treatment timing creates bias.

Assess:

- parallel trends,
- anticipation effects,
- treatment heterogeneity,
- spillovers into neighboring states,
- treatment contamination,
- illegal-market betting prior to legalization,
- COVID-era confounding,
- state-specific economic shocks,
- state gambling culture,
- concurrent marijuana or other vice legalization,
- and major changes in lottery/casino policy.

Identify the strongest plausible design, not the most complicated one.

# 11. Monetizing Social Costs

This is likely the hardest part of the project.

Before proposing a dollar-value social-cost measure, study the methodological literature on cost-of-gambling estimates.

Separate:

### Potentially legitimate external/public costs
Examples may include:

- publicly funded treatment,
- uncompensated healthcare,
- criminal justice costs,
- public assistance attributable to gambling-related financial distress,
- administrative costs,
- third-party family harms,
- productivity losses where economically justified,
- bankruptcy administrative costs,
- and other externally borne costs.

### Items that may NOT be social costs
Be cautious with:

- gambling losses themselves,
- transfers from bettor to sportsbook,
- voluntary consumption expenditures,
- private debt absent external effects,
- double-counting financial losses and downstream consequences,
- and speculative dollar valuations.

For every proposed cost measure, explain:

1. Why it constitutes a social/external cost.
2. Who bears the cost.
3. How it could be measured.
4. How it could be monetized.
5. Whether including it risks double counting.
6. Whether causal attribution to sports-betting legalization is realistic.

If full monetization cannot be defended, recommend a different design rather than forcing a dubious aggregate dollar estimate.

# 12. Alternative Research Designs

Do not become locked into one giant state-level cost-benefit paper.

After the literature and data audit, propose **3–5 viable project architectures**.

Possible examples:

### Design A — Net State Welfare/Fiscal Study
Compare gambling revenue with causally estimated monetizable external costs.

### Design B — Financial Well-Being Study
Focus tightly on consumer financial distress following mobile legalization.

### Design C — State Revenue Dependence / Consumer Protection
Examine whether states that become more fiscally dependent on gambling revenue adopt weaker consumer-protection rules or permit more aggressive marketing.

### Design D — Marketing Access and Vulnerable Consumers
Study whether mobile access and promotional intensity disproportionately affect vulnerable populations.

### Design E — Policy-Regime Heterogeneity
Compare states with different tax, advertising, promotional, operator, or responsible-gambling regimes and determine which structures reduce harm while preserving revenue.

These are only examples. Generate better alternatives if the literature suggests them.

For each proposed design, score:

- novelty,
- theoretical contribution,
- data availability,
- causal credibility,
- execution difficulty,
- publication potential,
- marketing relevance,
- and likely reviewer objections.

Use a 1–5 scale and explain each score briefly.
