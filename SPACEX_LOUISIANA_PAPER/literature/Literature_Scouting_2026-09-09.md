## 1. Claim specificity (carries over from, and complicates, the Tariff-paper attribution thread)

**Janssen, C., Swaen, V., & Du, S. (2022). Is a specific claim always better? The double-edged
effects of claim specificity in green advertising. *Journal of Business Research*, 151, 435–447.**
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.07.005 — **PRIMARY TIER**, full text directly fetched and
read (`pdftotext`, not a search snippet or AI synthesis).

Two experiments show claim specificity in CSR/green advertising is *not* uniformly better: its
positive effect depends on the brand's perceived competence, and when both brand warmth and
competence are low, a specific claim can backfire — increasing perceived manipulative intent and
lowering attitudes/purchase intent, serially mediated through perceived manipulative intent and
perceived environmental commitment. Also cites a 2020 European Commission study finding 42% of
green online claims were exaggerated, false, or deceptive, and notes FTC/EU regulators treat vague
green claims with particular suspicion.

**Why this matters for this project, specifically:** the existing framing (Musk's round "10,000
jobs" = vague vs. LED's precise "3,000+8,100" = specific) implicitly treats specificity as simply
"more credible." This paper is a direct, citable complication: specificity's credibility payoff is
conditional on the *source's* perceived competence/warmth — worth building into whichever framing
Britton eventually picks, rather than treating claim-specificity as a context-free good.

## 2. Symbolic vs. substantive environmental commitment ("greenwashing" mechanism)

**Kudłak, R. (2025). Greenwashing or Striving to Persist: An Alternative Explanation of a Loose
Coupling Between Corporate Environmental Commitments and Outcomes. *Journal of Business Ethics*,
197(2), 355–370.** DOI 10.1007/s10551-024-05778-w — **SEARCH-SNIPPET TIER.** Bibliographic detail
(journal/volume/issue/pages) confirmed via the article's own ProQuest preview page; full
text/abstract is paywalled (Springer login-gated, SAGE-style access wall) and was not read
directly. Single-author attribution to Kudłak came from one AI-search synthesis only — **flag for
independent verification before citing**, per this project's own repeated caution about
AI-search-fabricated specifics.

Per the ProQuest preview and secondary characterization: argues the gap between stated corporate
environmental commitments and actual outcomes ("loose coupling") isn't necessarily deliberate
greenwashing — it can also reflect genuine implementation difficulty (causal indeterminacy,
fragmented internal/external environments). **Why this matters:** a useful theoretical guardrail
against assuming SpaceX's own claim-outcome gaps (once Boca Chica-style outcomes exist for
Louisiana) are automatically bad-faith — this paper gives citable language for an alternative,
more defensible explanation the manuscript should at least address rather than ignore.

## 3. Regulatory venue shopping (carries over from Data Center Paper's established framing)

**Coen, D., Guidi, M., Yordanova, N., & Héritier, A. (2020). The logic of regulatory venue
shopping: A firm's perspective. *Public Policy and Administration*, 36(3), 323–342.** —
**PRIMARY TIER**, directly fetched from the journal's own page (via proxy after a direct 403).

Survey of 243 medium/large firms across five regulated sectors in 29 European countries. Core
finding: firms strategically target the regulatory venue where "political and regulatory
competence lies" (national level dominant in this sample), and venue-shopping intensifies when
issues are highly salient, highly technical, and high-stakes. **Why this matters:** gives an
empirical, survey-based anchor (not just case narrative) for the paper's regulatory-venue-shifting
thread — the FAA environmental-review-waiver push running parallel to SpaceX's own voluntary
environmental-commitment language is a clean fit for "moving the fight to the venue where the
firm's position is strongest."
