## 2. Acevedo, Fischhoff & Patrício (2026) — priority item 2

**Result: VERIFIED, with an important nuance/partial correction to the audit's stated ranking
finding.**

**Crossref-confirmed citation:** Brissa Acevedo, Baruch Fischhoff, & Lia Patrício, "Community
preferences in energy justice: Perspectives on solar farm projects," *Energy Research & Social
Science*, 135, article 104667 (2026). DOI: `10.1016/j.erss.2026.104667`. Published online
2026-03-22, print issue dated 2026-05. Open access, CC-BY-NC-ND license per Crossref metadata (a
weaker open-access license than Oliveira 2026's CC-BY, worth noting if anyone later tries a
full-text pull — NC-ND permits reading but restricts reuse). All three author names, title, and
journal match the audit's placeholder citation exactly — this resolves the audit's "[Justice
predictors of community acceptance for benefit-sharing infrastructure]" bracketed placeholder
title into a real, citable title.

**Content check (search-summarized-only, corroborated across two independent WebSearch queries
both pointing to the same ScienceDirect abstract):** The study uses a multi-method approach
assessing the relative importance of **four justice dimensions — Recognition, Distributional,
Procedural, and Restorative** — via variants of a Community Benefits Agreement (CBA) for a
**solar farm development sited in Maui, Hawaii** (not a general infrastructure/benefit-sharing
study — it is one specific solar-siting case). Each CBA variant added benefits tied to one
dimension onto a baseline plan, and researchers measured which addition most changed stakeholder
preference/acceptance.

**Nuance on the ranking (this is the one place this session's finding differs from the audit's
stated claim, not a fabrication but a precision issue worth flagging plainly):** The audit's
citation (#16, and the ChatGPT review package) states the ranking as "recognitive > restorative >
distributional > procedural" — a fully strict four-way ordering. The most detailed WebSearch
summary obtained this session instead describes it as: **Recognition Justice had the greatest
impact; Restorative Justice had the next greatest impact; Procedural and Distributional Justice
benefits followed and were "similar" to each other** — i.e., the paper's own reported result does
not cleanly rank distributional above procedural (or vice versa) at the bottom of the order; those
two were roughly tied. The top of the ranking (Recognition first, Restorative second) matches the
audit exactly. This is a secondary-source (WebSearch-summarized) characterization, not a read of
the full paper's results table, so it should be treated as the current best available read rather
than a certainty — but it is corroborated identically across two independently-phrased searches
both citing the same underlying ScienceDirect abstract, which is a decent (though not primary-
document) level of confidence for a nuance this specific.

**Bearing on the audit's Section 3/8 argument (reporting only, not deciding):** The audit uses this
paper to argue the manuscript's two-mediator (distributive + procedural) model omits recognition
and restorative justice, which this paper finds rank higher. That argument is not weakened by
this session's finding — if anything the "distributional and procedural were statistically similar
to each other, and both were outranked by recognition and restorative justice" version is an
even sharper version of the audit's complication, since it removes any basis for treating
procedural as clearly more (or less) impactful than distributional in this specific dataset. This
stays exactly the same scope decision the audit already flagged as Britton's call — reported here
as a literature-input correction, not a modeling recommendation.

**Status change: LIKELY-REAL → VERIFIED (Crossref-verified bibliographic; search-summarized-only
content, with a ranking nuance to flag if this citation is used to state the specific order rather
than just "recognition and restorative outrank the other two").**
