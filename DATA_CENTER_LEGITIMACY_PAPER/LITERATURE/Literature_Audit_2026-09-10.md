## 1. Executive Assessment

**How defensible is BBA right now?** Partially, and less than the current framing assumes. The core intuition — diffuse societal benefits, concentrated local burdens — is not new. It is a ~45-year-old finding in political economy (Wilson's 1980 concentrated-cost/diffuse-benefit framework, building on Olson's collective-action logic) and is substantially covered by the energy-justice tradition (Walker & Devine-Wright 2008; McCauley, Heffron, Stephan & Jenkins 2013; Jenkins et al. 2016) and by spatial justice (Soja). BBA's real, defensible novelty is narrower than "we found that benefits and burdens are unevenly distributed": it is (a) naming and operationalizing this as a multidimensional diagnostic construct, (b) explicitly separating an *objective/structural* form from a *perceived/evaluative* form, and (c) applying it to a genuinely new empirical domain — AI/data-center infrastructure — and to *corporate* (not just governmental) legitimacy. That is a real contribution, but the paper needs to state it exactly this way, or a reviewer will state the broader, weaker version for you.

**Biggest novelty threat:** Two convergent bodies of prior art, found independently by two separate search passes: Wilson's (1980) diffuse-benefit/concentrated-cost framework, and the energy-justice distributive/procedural tradition. A CSREM reviewer with any environmental- or energy-justice background will very likely say "this is distributive (in)justice with a new name." Two of the four BBA dimensions have exact-term collisions with established literatures: "scale mismatch" already means something specific and different in environmental governance (Cash et al. 2006; Cumming, Cumming & Redman 2006 — two independent 2006 papers in *Ecology and Society* using this precise term for institutional/ecological scale-fit, not benefit-vs-burden scale), and "institutional distance" already means something specific and different in international-business/cross-border research (Kostova's construct: regulatory/normative/cultural distance between countries).

**Strongest part of the theory:** P4, procedural justice → legitimacy/social license. This is backed by a true meta-analysis (Walters & Bolger 2019, *Journal of Experimental Criminology*, 64 studies/196 effect sizes, DOI 10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2 — cross-domain but methodologically the strongest anchor available) plus domain-matched infrastructure/mining evidence (Feng, Liu & Ge 2024, already in the project file; Moffat & Zhang 2014). P3 (distributive justice → legitimacy) is nearly as strong, anchored directly by Moffat & Zhang's (2014) integrative mining-SLO model — the closest existing empirical test of this paper's exact causal chain, just in a different industry.

**Weakest theoretical relationship:** P2, BBA → procedural justice — exactly as the task brief suspected, and confirmed independently by both the novelty-search and proposition-search passes. The organizational-justice literature (Colquitt et al. 2001 meta-analysis) and a distributive/procedural-justice correlation meta-analysis (lead found, author unconfirmed — see Section 8) show DJ and PJ perceptions as strongly correlated and mutually reinforcing rather than cleanly sequential. The "fair process effect" literature (Van den Bos and colleagues) runs partly in the *opposite* direction — perceived procedural fairness shapes acceptance of unequal outcomes, not the reverse. As worded, P2 risks a discriminant-validity objection (are BBA→DJ and BBA→PJ really two independent paths, or one path measured twice?).

**Is the paper still viable for CSREM?** Yes, conditionally. It needs to reposition as a corporate-legitimacy/social-license contribution (CSREM's actual home turf — Corporate Digital Responsibility, stakeholder theory, legitimacy-under-controversy are all active CSREM themes, per Zarzycka 2025, Jansen et al. 2024, Castilho Rossoni 2026) rather than reading as an environmental-justice-theory paper that landed in the wrong journal. Right now the model is justice/legitimacy-centric with the firm almost invisible; strengthening the corporate-response layer (site selection, benefit design, communication quality — Feng, Liu & Ge 2024's finding that informal enterprise treatment outweighs formal government process is the natural anchor) is what makes this read as CSREM rather than *Energy Research & Social Science*. The good news: no dedicated CSREM — or peer-reviewed — article on data centers/AI infrastructure specifically was found in any of the three passes. That gap is real and is the paper's cleanest, most defensible novelty claim.

**Top 5 changes to make now:**
1. Add an explicit "how BBA differs from Wilson's (1980) framework and the energy-justice distributive tenet" subsection in Theoretical Foundations — name the prior art before a reviewer does.
2. Rework P2. Either soften it to a correlational/joint-perception claim, reframe the mechanism as "BBA heightens scrutiny of decision-making process" rather than "directly reduces perceived procedural fairness," or fold DJ/PJ into a single perceived-injustice construct with the DJ/PJ split treated as a boundary condition, not a given.
3. Rename two constructs: "scale mismatch" (collides with Cash et al. 2006 / Cumming, Cumming & Redman 2006's governance-scale-mismatch construct) and "institutional distance from burden" (collides with Kostova's IB-literature "institutional distance," and duplicates BBA's own geographic-dispersion dimension if left as a distance/geography framing). Recommended replacement for the latter: **decision-maker proximity**.
4. Reground "benefit localization" explicitly in the established benefit-sharing / community benefit agreement (CBA) literature rather than presenting it as a new moderator — cite Esteves & Vanclay, De Barbieri, and the CBA/energy-justice literature by name.
5. Add the corporate/firm-level layer explicitly (site-selection strategy, benefit design, communication quality) so the manuscript reads as a CSREM contribution — Feng, Liu & Ge (2024) and Calvano (2008), already in the project file, are the right anchors; use them centrally, not as background.

**Direct verdict:** BBA is not novel as a raw idea — the diffuse-benefit/concentrated-burden intuition is well established across at least three literatures (political economy, energy justice, spatial justice). ## 3. Proposition-by-Proposition Literature Audit

### P1. BBA → distributive justice
**Current logic:** Structural benefit/burden asymmetry reduces perceived fairness of outcomes.
**Best supporting literature:** The energy-justice distributive tenet treats unequal distribution as close to definitional of low distributive-justice perception (McCauley et al. 2013; Jenkins et al. 2016). Been (1994) supplies the LULU-siting/environmental-justice legal anchor. Klein (2008, already in project file) gives the macromarketing benefit-cost framing.
**Gaps:** Almost no study operationalizes an *asymmetry* construct (objective + perceived, multidimensional) as the independent variable — most studies measure "unequal distribution" directly, which risks collapsing BBA and low-DJ into the same variable empirically. This is a measurement-independence problem a reviewer will likely raise.
**Recommended citations:** Been, J.L. (1994), "What's Fairness Got to Do With It? Environmental Justice and the Siting of Locally Undesirable Land Uses," *Cornell Law Review* 78(6) [LIKELY-REAL — verify exact title/volume]; Walker & Devine-Wright (2008); Klein (2008, existing).
**Wording change?** Yes — add an explicit sentence distinguishing BBA (structural/perceptual antecedent) from the distributive-justice judgment (evaluative outcome), with a falsifiable prediction that separates them (e.g., BBA should predict DJ net of raw benefit/cost magnitude).

### P2. BBA → procedural justice
**Current logic:** Structural asymmetry reduces perceived fairness of the decision-making process.
**Best supporting literature:** Weaker than P1, and the weakest link in the whole model — confirmed independently by two search passes. Political-trust research (*Distributive and Procedural Justice and Political Trust in Europe*, Frontiers in Political Science) shows distributive and procedural shortfalls as mutually reinforcing rather than cleanly sequential. Wind/solar siting case studies show residents who perceive unequal outcomes retroactively reinterpret the *process* as unfair (procedural injustice as a consequence of, or co-occurring with, distributive injustice — not cleanly the reverse).
**Gaps:** A distributive/procedural-justice correlation meta-analysis was found by title but not confirmed by author/journal — flagged high-priority to track down, because if it shows a strong positive correlation (as Colquitt et al. 2001's organizational-justice meta-analysis would predict, ρ reported around .6 in the adjacent literature), that directly challenges modeling DJ and PJ as two independently-caused mediators. The "fair-process effect" literature (Van den Bos et al.) runs partly in the *opposite* direction from what P2 needs.
**Recommended citations:** Colquitt, Conlon, Wesson, Porter & Ng (2001), "Justice at the millennium," *Journal of Applied Psychology* 86(3):425-445 [LIKELY-REAL, canonical] — cite to engage the correlation problem directly, not just to support the arrow.
**Wording change?** Yes, and treat as a real boundary-condition risk — either soften to "BBA heightens scrutiny of decision-making process" (a distinct, plausible mechanism: visible asymmetry makes people look harder at *how* a decision was made, rather than directly reducing perceived fairness of that process), or explicitly model DJ and PJ as correlated co-outcomes rather than fully independent paths.

### P3. Distributive justice → legitimacy / social license
**Best supporting literature:** Moffat & Zhang (2014), *Resources Policy* 39:61-70 — an empirically tested integrative model linking DJ and PJ (via trust) to SLO/community acceptance in mining; the closest existing empirical analog to this paper's exact causal chain, just in a different industry. Gross (2007), *Energy Policy* — highly cited (700+ citations) wind-energy community-perceptions study, infrastructure-specific. Acevedo, Fischhoff & Patrício (2026) find distributive justice is one of several significant predictors of community acceptance for infrastructure with benefit-sharing — but their ranking (recognitive > restorative > distributional > procedural) complicates a two-mediator model.
**Gaps:** The Acevedo et al. finding is a real complication — if recognition and restorative justice outrank distributive/procedural justice empirically, a reviewer may ask why the model carries only two of at least four established justice tenets.
**Recommended citations:** Moffat & Zhang (2014) — should become one of the most-cited papers in the manuscript; Gross (2007); Acevedo, Fischhoff & Patrício (2026).
**Wording change?** Consider explicitly bounding scope — state why recognition/restorative justice are excluded (parsimony, or folded into DJ/PJ) rather than silently omitting them.

### P4. Procedural justice → legitimacy / social license
**Best supporting literature:** The strongest-evidenced link in the model. Walters & Bolger (2019), *Journal of Experimental Criminology*, DOI 10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2 — a true meta-analysis (64 studies, 95 samples, 196 effect sizes, 1990–2018) confirming PJ→legitimacy as consistently positive and strong. Domain is policing/compliance, not infrastructure, but it is the best available meta-analytic backbone and can be cited cross-domain. Domain-matched support: Feng, Liu & Ge (2024, existing anchor), Xu, Cui, Liu & Skitmore (2023), *Land Use Policy* [LIKELY-REAL], and Moffat & Zhang (2014) replicate the direction specifically in infrastructure/mining/siting contexts. Tyler's (1990/2006) procedural-justice/legitimacy theory is the originating foundational source and is currently absent from the citation base entirely.
**Gaps:** None major. Main risk is citing only single infrastructure studies without the meta-analytic backbone.
**Recommended citations:** Walters & Bolger (2019); Tyler, T.R. (1990/2006), *Why People Obey the Law*, Princeton Univ. Press [LIKELY-REAL, foundational — should be added regardless of other choices]; Feng, Liu & Ge (2024, existing); Moffat & Zhang (2014).
**Wording change?** No — this proposition is in good shape; strengthen the citation stack rather than the wording.

### P5. Benefit localization (moderator)
**Current logic:** Localizing benefits (jobs, tax revenue, local ownership) weakens the negative BBA → DJ relationship.
**Best supporting literature:** Direct empirical tests exist. Acevedo, Fischhoff & Patrício (2026) test benefit-sharing structures against justice perceptions directly. A 2025 *Energy Policy* community-benefit-funds study (MaREI/EirGrid grid-infrastructure case) tests whether routing tangible local benefits to host communities changes engagement/acceptance — close to a direct test of this exact moderator [LIKELY-REAL — verify before citing]. The community benefit agreement (CBA) literature (Esteves & Vanclay 2009; De Barbieri, *Pace Environmental Law Review* 42(1)) is the mechanism-level literature this construct should be built from.
**Verdict on novelty:** Not novel as a standalone construct. The literature already has a name for this mechanism — **benefit-sharing** — with local ownership, local procurement, and host-community compensation as recognized sub-mechanisms.
**Recommended citations:** Esteves & Vanclay (2009), *Environmental Impact Assessment Review* 29(2):137-145 [LIKELY-REAL]; De Barbieri, *Pace Environmental Law Review* 42(1) [LIKELY-REAL]; Acevedo, Fischhoff & Patrício (2026).
**Wording change?** Yes, partially. Either rename to "benefit-sharing" / "local benefit-sharing" to align with the established literature, or keep "benefit localization" as a manuscript-specific label but state explicitly in-text that it is an application of the benefit-sharing/CBA construct, narrowed to the spatial co-location of benefit and burden — not a free-standing new moderator.

### P6. Institutional distance from burden (moderator)
**Current logic:** Greater distance between the institution deciding and the community bearing the burden strengthens the negative BBA → PJ relationship.
**Aggressive scrutiny result:** No literature uses the exact phrase "institutional distance from burden." Several partial matches cluster around the same idea: the **subsidiarity principle** (decisions should occur at the lowest appropriate level; normative/institutional-design theory, not an empirical moderator) is the closest pre-existing theoretical home and supplies the vocabulary almost verbatim ("proximity of decision-makers to the affected population"). **Fiscal federalism** (Oates 1972) gives the classical economic-efficiency version of the same argument. **Multilevel governance / regulatory federalism / center-local conflict** supports the general phenomenon without a single agreed construct name. **Construal-level theory / psychological distance** (used in CSR-judgment research) is the strongest candidate for the individual-perception *mechanism* — why distance from a decision-maker would change a resident's fairness judgment, as opposed to just describing the governance structure.
**Naming recommendation:** Rename. "Institutional distance" is already a specific, heavily used term in international-business/cross-border research (Kostova's construct: regulatory/normative/cultural distance between countries) — a direct cross-disciplinary collision. It also risks duplicating BBA's own "geographic dispersion" dimension if framed geographically (a reviewer could reasonably ask "isn't this just Dimension 1 again?"). Recommend **decision-maker proximity** (inverse-coded: low proximity = high distance) over the task's other alternatives (governance-burden distance, authority-burden distance, representational distance) because "proximity" is the term the closest adjacent literatures (subsidiarity, accountability, CLT) actually converge on, and it is cleanly measurable via direct survey items ("how much say did decision-makers who live near me have, versus distant regulators/executives").
**Recommended citations:** Oates (1972); the subsidiarity-principle literature; construal-level-theory/psychological-distance-in-CSR-judgment literature [LIKELY-REAL — verify specific citation]; two empirical leads on administrative/accountability distance were found but not confirmed by author/journal this session — **re-pull before use.**

## 4. BBA Dimension Audit

**1. Geographic dispersion** — Strong, direct precedent: spatial justice (Soja 2010) frames distributive spatial justice in language that is close to a verbatim match ("how the burdens and benefits of collective life are geographically distributed"). **Status:** solid, but arguably a restatement of spatial justice's core claim applied to a single facility rather than an original dimension. **Recommendation:** Retain, but cite Soja explicitly as ancestor literature rather than presenting it as original; consider "spatial concentration of burden" as a more precisely scoped label.

**2. Scale mismatch** — Direct, exact-term collision, corroborated independently by two search passes and two separate 2006 papers in the same journal: Cash et al. (2006) and Cumming, Cumming & Redman (2006), both *Ecology and Society*, both using "scale mismatch" for institutional/ecological scale-fit (the scale of a governing institution vs. the scale of the process it manages) — not benefit-scale vs. burden-scale. Cumming et al. additionally note mismatches are often "produced and maintained by powerful actors who benefit from... externalization of costs across scales," which is conceptually adjacent but not identical to this dimension's intended meaning. **Status:** riskiest dimension as currently labeled. **Recommendation:** Revise the label (e.g., "magnitude mismatch" or "jurisdictional scale gap") while keeping the substantive idea, and cite Cash et al./Cumming et al. explicitly to distinguish usage rather than risk a reviewer citing them against the paper.

**3. Visibility asymmetry** — No construct-level precedent under this exact name; two partial, non-fused literatures. Star (1999), "The Ethnography of Infrastructure," *American Behavioral Scientist* 43(3):377-391 [VERIFIED, well-cited STS classic] establishes that infrastructure is characteristically invisible until it breaks down, and that visibility is relational (infrastructure for one group, barrier for another). A Schattschneider-lineage political-visibility argument (concentrated/visible costs vs. diffuse/invisible benefits shaping political salience) was found only via secondary sources this session — **UNVERIFIED at primary-source level**, needs a direct check before citing. Real empirical traction exists in the data-center corpus itself: NDAs, undisclosed end-users, and closed-door negotiations are reportedly a dominant complaint in actual data-center opposition cases (per Priority 3 findings, Section 6) — this dimension has case support even without a clean theoretical home yet. **Status:** theoretically thinnest of the four, but empirically live. **Recommendation:** Revise — keep the dimension, but ground it explicitly in Star (1999) plus a verified Schattschneider-lineage primary source, or reframe as a manifestation of corporate-secrecy/disclosure practices rather than a co-equal structural dimension.

**4. Temporal mismatch** — Real precedent exists, but almost entirely in intergenerational/climate-justice literature (costs now, harms later, at societal/planetary scale) — a different temporal logic and scale than "this facility's benefits arrive on a different timeline than this community's burdens" (more a project-lifecycle/permitting-timeline claim: burdens front-loaded during construction/operation, benefits speculative or deferred). No literature was found applying temporal mismatch at facility-siting scale specifically. **Status:** the dimension with the most legitimate claim to genuine, if modest, novelty — precisely because the existing temporal-justice literature operates at a different scale and hasn't been scaled down. **Recommendation:** Retain, cite the intergenerational/climate-justice literature explicitly as ancestor theory, and frame the novel move as an explicit scale-extension (global/intergenerational → single-facility/project-lifecycle), not as an independently discovered phenomenon. Consider whether it functions better as a boundary condition (BBA effects intensify when burdens are front-loaded and benefits are deferred/speculative) than as a coequal fourth diagnostic dimension.

**Overall dimension-structure recommendation:** The four dimensions are unevenly grounded — do not present them as a symmetric, equally novel typology. Geographic dispersion and scale mismatch are close to relabeled prior art (spatial justice; governance scale-mismatch, respectively); visibility asymmetry is theoretically thin but empirically supported in-corpus; temporal mismatch is the most defensible as a genuine scale-extension. Recommend the manuscript explicitly separate **inherited/relabeled dimensions** (geographic dispersion, scale mismatch) from **the paper's actual contribution** (temporal mismatch at facility scale; visibility asymmetry as applied specifically to AI/data-center corporate-secrecy practices) — a more defensible framing than presenting all four as an original typology invented whole-cloth.

## 5. Construct Naming Audit

- **Benefit–burden asymmetry (BBA):** Keep the label, revise the claim. Position explicitly as extending Wilson's (1980) diffuse-benefit/concentrated-cost framework and the energy-justice distributive tradition into a named, multidimensional, infrastructure-specific diagnostic — not as an original discovery of uneven benefit/burden distribution.
- **Benefit localization:** Keep the label only if explicitly defined in-text as an application of the established benefit-sharing/community-benefit-agreement literature (Esteves & Vanclay; De Barbieri), narrowed to the spatial co-location of benefit and burden. Otherwise, rename to "benefit-sharing" or "local benefit-sharing" to align directly with the literature it draws from.
- **Institutional distance from burden:** Rename. Both independent search passes converged on the same recommendation: **decision-maker proximity** (inverse-coded), avoiding the direct naming collision with international-business "institutional distance" (Kostova) and the redundancy risk with BBA's own geographic-dispersion dimension. If a distance-framed (rather than proximity-framed) label is preferred for manuscript-internal consistency, "governance–burden distance" is the next-best option — but "institutional distance" specifically should not be retained.

## 9. Recommended Manuscript Changes

- **Introduction:** Open by naming Wilson's (1980) framework and the energy-justice tradition directly; state the gap plainly — neither has produced a named, multidimensional diagnostic applied to AI/data-center infrastructure and corporate legitimacy specifically. Position against Jansen et al. (2024)'s CSREM research agenda for controversial-industry theory.
- **Theoretical Foundations:** Add a subsection explicitly distinguishing BBA from (a) distributive injustice, (b) energy justice's distributive tenet, and (c) Wilson's framework. Add Tyler (1990/2006) and Gehman, Lefsrud & Fast (2017) wherever legitimacy/SLO is defined.
- **BBA section:** Revise "scale mismatch" terminology (cite Cash et al./Cumming et al. to disambiguate); reconsider whether visibility asymmetry and temporal mismatch are coequal dimensions or mechanisms/boundary conditions; ground visibility asymmetry in Star (1999).
- **Justice sections:** Reframe P2 per Section 3 — either soften the causal claim or explicitly engage the DJ/PJ correlation literature (Colquitt et al. 2001) so the two-mediator structure survives a discriminant-validity critique. Add Moffat & Zhang (2014) and Walters & Bolger (2019) as primary empirical anchors for P3/P4. Explicitly bound scope against the four-tenet (not two-tenet) energy-justice framework (Acevedo et al. 2026).
- **Boundary Conditions:** Rename "institutional distance from burden" to "decision-maker proximity"; reground "benefit localization" in the benefit-sharing/CBA literature explicitly.
- **Conceptual Model:** Consider restructuring the four BBA dimensions to visually/formally separate inherited/relabeled dimensions (geographic dispersion, scale mismatch) from the paper's actual contribution (temporal mismatch at facility scale, visibility asymmetry applied to AI/data-center secrecy practices).
- **Discussion:** Engage the Gehman, Lefsrud & Fast (2017) "SLO = legitimacy?" debate explicitly; discuss the recognition/restorative-justice omission (Acevedo et al. 2026) as a scope decision, not an oversight.
- **Theoretical Contributions:** State the narrower, defensible novelty claim explicitly (multidimensional diagnostic + new empirical domain + corporate/CSREM framing), not the broader "benefits and burdens are unevenly distributed" claim the current framing implies.
- **Future Research:** Flag the corporate/firm-level layer (site-selection strategy, benefit design, communication quality, per Feng, Liu & Ge 2024) as the CSREM-native extension distinguishing this from an environmental-justice-journal contribution.
