Abstract
In recent years, artificial intelligence has developed into a powerful tool for processing 
and generating human-like texts, unlocking innovative possibilities for quantitative and 
qualitative data analysis. Within qualitative research, artificial intelligence in general, and 
ChatGPT in particular, represent promising avenues to explore and examine textual tran￾scriptions from interview data. This study is a step forward in this direction, advancing a 
reliable research protocol for using ChatGPT to conduct thematic analysis, which includes 
the following standard steps: (1) data preparation, (2) defining the analysis process, (3) 
chatbot interaction, (4) iterative process, (5) review and validation, and (6) analysis and 
interpretation. Results of the analysis revealed that ChatGPT may significantly facilitate 
qualitative data analysis exploration, especially during initial research stages and when 
dealing with extensive transcription material. Additionally, our protocol design is able to 
reliably identify different thematic patterns emerging from the text, although the granular￾ity of the output may vary depending on the quality of the prompt and human intelligence 
interpretation. Accordingly, we conclude that despite its vast power, the ChatGPT model 
in its current state is unable to substitute the contextual insights and subtle metaphorical 
nuances associated with human qualitative analysis, interpretation and reflexivity.

In recent years, the advent of new technologies has significantly transformed methodol￾ogies for data analysis, expanding the range of tools available to researchers across dis￾ciplines (e.g., Hariri 2023; Mohd Zabidi et al. 2022; Morgan 2023; Gil de Zúñiga et al. 
2024). Among these innovations, ChatGPT—a state-of-the-art language model developed 
by OpenAI—has emerged as a powerful resource capable of processing and generating 
human-like text (Hariri 2023; Lingard 2023). According to recent research (De Paoli and 
Mathis 2024; Kumar 2023; Sallam 2023; Shtykalo and Yamnenko 2024), its application in 
qualitative studies, particularly in coding and thematic analysis, has gained attention for its 
potential to streamline the analytical process. In fact, a number of studies have suggested 
that when employed ethically and under human supervision (Zhang et  al. 2024), Chat￾GPT can assist in generating initial codes or providing alternative interpretations, thereby 
enhancing the efficiency and depth of analysis (Morgan 2023).
Qualitative research is particularly valued for its ability to capture the depth and 
nuances of human experiences, behaviors, and interactions (Taylor et al. 2015; Lapan 
et al. 2011). Unlike quantitative approaches, qualitative research relies on interpretative 
methodologies that enable researchers to explore complex real-world contexts (Ham￾mersley 2012; Lacey and Luff 2001; Pilcher and Cortazzi 2024; Thompson 2017). This 
methodological approach plays a crucial role in theory development by illustrating the 
nuances of culture, identity, and social structures (Patton 2014; Taylor et  al. 2015). 
Within this broader qualitative framework, thematic analysis has emerged as one of the 
most widely used methods for systematically identifying, analyzing, and reporting pat￾terns or themes in textual data (Braun and Clarke 2006; Byrne 2022; Javadi and Zarea 
2016). Taken together, extant research has extensively discussed and empirically dem￾onstrated the flexibility of thematic analysis in deriving meaningful insights into partici￾pants’ experiences, perceptions, and perspectives (Vaismoradi et al. 2016).
In this context, the integration of ChatGPT into thematic analysis presents novel 
possibilities for enhancing qualitative research. Researchers can now leverage artificial 
intelligence to navigate complex and voluminous datasets more efficiently (De Paoli and 
Mathis 2024; Morgan 2023). Specifically, ChatGPT can fulfill at least four key roles 
in thematic analysis. First, it can standardize the coding process by reducing the vari￾ability introduced by manual coding performed by human teams, thereby promoting 
consistency across analyses (De Paoli and Mathis 2024; Zhang et al. 2024). Second, it 
can minimize individual biases that may inadvertently shape research outcomes (Yan 
et  al. 2024). By automating the initial stages of interpretation, ChatGPT supports a 
more objective preliminary analysis, independent of prior human assumptions. Third, 
it can enhance efficiency by rapidly processing large volumes of textual data, allowing 
researchers to allocate more time to interpretative synthesis (Zhang et al. 2023). Finally, 
ChatGPT can identify emerging themes that might not be immediately apparent, uncov￾ering latent patterns that enrich the analytical process (Aboalela 2023).
Despite these advantages, the complexities associated with AI-assisted thematic 
analysis underscore the need for a structured research protocol to ensure its methodo￾logical soundness and ethical integrity. This study addresses this critical gap by pre￾senting a practical, step-by-step protocol for incorporating ChatGPT into thematic 
analysis. By systematically exploring the intersection of artificial intelligence and quali￾tative research, this paper aims to bridge the gap between human critical thinking and 
AI-assisted thematic analysis. In doing so, it seeks to equip researchers with a clearer 
understanding of ChatGPT’s capabilities and limitations, fostering its responsible use in 
qualitative inquiry.

1  Qualitative research and data analysis
Qualitative research encompasses a wide range of data collection techniques that aim to 
provide a detailed and socio-contextual interpretation of social phenomena, such as in￾depth interviews, focus groups or participant observation (Vaismoradi et al. 2016). These 
methods are conducted in a flexible, open-ended manner, drawing on rich narrative mate￾rial that allows researchers to get to know the interpretations, thoughts and feelings of the 
people studied in an intimate way (Denscombe 2009; Patton 2014; Pilcher and Cortazzi 
2024). When analyzing data, qualitative researchers develop concepts, categories and 
insights from the patterns in data, which are also flexible and open-ended in character 
(Hammersley 2012; Taylor et al. 2015).
Qualitative research emerged as an anti-positivist movement that rejected quantitative 
methods and their reduction of social reality to variables and that moved instead towards 
theorizing (Alasuutari 2010). It is often associated with the interpretivist paradigm and has 
sometimes been labeled “unscientific” by some quantitative scholars due to its lack of gen￾eralizability (Adler 2022; Pilcher and Cortazzi 2024; Sarma 2015). However, qualitative 
methods are frequently used in contemporary research and their importance for a more 
comprehensive understanding of social and cultural phenomena is often acknowledged 
(Mohajan 2018). Unlike quantitative research, whose strength lies in its ability to gener￾alize and replicate data in other settings, qualitative methods have the unique ability to 
develop new theoretical insights. Moreover, by examining the context of social phenom￾ena and the meanings people attach to them, qualitative methods reach the parts of knowl￾edge that quantitative methods cannot. They investigate the questions of “how” and “why” 
rather than “when”,” “what” and “where” (Creswell and Poth 2016; Thorogood and Green 
2018). These methods therefore examine phenomena as a whole and not as individual vari￾ables, which leads to a complex and holistic picture of them (Taylor et al. 2015).
The process of qualitative data analysis follows inductive reasoning. This means that 
qualitative researchers do not base their analysis on existing theories, ideas and hypotheses, 
as is the case with quantitative methods, but begin by examining the empirical observa￾tions they have collected (Madondo 2021; Mohd Zabidi et al. 2022). This means that they 
first delve into participants’ “thick descriptions”—their “views, intents, circumstances, 
motives, meanings, and understandings” (Younas et al. 2023, p. 1)—and begin to think in 
increasingly abstract ways until their vague ideas become precise theoretical concepts and 
propositions (Neuman 2019).
Within this framework, qualitative data analysis has been described as one of the most 
challenging parts of qualitative research (Castleberry and Nolen 2018; O’Kane et al. 2021). 
Very often, qualitative researchers are faced with large amounts of textual data to analyze, 
not quite knowing how to proceed. This occurs because, unlike in quantitative methods, 
there are no explicit rules for data analysis in qualitative research (Graue 2015; Mohd 
Zabidi et al. 2022). The confusion about this process is compounded by the fact that quali￾tative researchers generally do not describe their analytic processes very clearly or sys￾tematically (Kekez 2019; Tuckett 2005). Analysis tends to be murky and, as a result, some 
scholars have questioned its rigor, sometimes accusing qualitative scholars of adopting 
an “anything goes” mentality (Braun and Clarke 2006; O’Kane et al. 2021; Sarma 2015). 
However, when done right, the flexible nature of the qualitative approach does not preclude 
it from being rigorous and systematic.
Accordingly, qualitative scholars have focused for many years on developing 
guidelines and techniques to analyze and elucidate patterns in textual data in a more

standardized way. Some of these techniques are widely used, such as grounded theory 
(Corbin and Strauss 1990), narrative analysis (Polkinghorne 1995) or thematic analy￾sis (Braun and Clarke 2006). The latter has become one of the best-known and most 
popular analytical strategies in recent decades, which can be traced back to the much￾cited methodological paper by Braun and Clarke (2006). Thematic analysis is a method 
for identifying, analyzing and reporting themes in data and is described as “the more 
systematic and explicit form” of data analysis in qualitative research (Javadi and Zarea 
2016, p. 30). It ensures standardization and transparency while maintaining the flex￾ibility that is so characteristic of qualitative methods.
Braun and Clarke (2006) proposed a six-phase guideline for it. These six stages 
provide a systematic but theoretically open-ended procedure for coding and examining 
descriptions through the discovery of themes (Braun and Clarke 2006; Byrne 2022; 
Vaismoradi et  al. 2016). This analytic procedure goes as follows. First, researchers 
need to familiarize themselves with their data by transcribing, reading and re-reading 
it and writing down initial ideas. Second, they have to generate initial codes for the 
entire data set and select data relevant to the codes. Next, researchers need to look for 
themes by finding common patterns in codes. Themes may be described as “the sub￾jective meaning and cultural-contextual message of data” (Vaismoradi and Snelgrove 
2019, p. 2) and are the end product of thematic analysis. Researchers must then review, 
define and name themes, and finally, conduct a final analysis and write the report.
In addition to introducing clearer guidelines for analyzing textual data, qualitative 
scholars have increasingly integrated new technologies into these processes. Computer￾based programs such as NVivo or Atlas.ti are now commonly used by researchers and 
serve as tools to find, categorize, and retrieve data much faster than manual searching 
(Liamputtong 2009). These programs support and enhance the overall analysis pro￾cess and allow for more complex and in-depth analysis of data, especially in studies 
working with large datasets (Castleberry and Nolen 2018; Hwang 2008; Liamputtong 
2009). More importantly, they are proving useful in demonstrating more transparent 
and trustworthy work (O’Kane et al. 2021).
Now, recent technological advances in the field of artificial intelligence have opened 
up new possibilities for the systematization and transparency of qualitative research. 
This is the case with ChatGPT, the state-of-the-art language model developed by Ope￾nAI, which is gradually being implemented in analytical procedures, research and even 
writing. Although some concerns have been raised about its use in research and the 
risk of bias, plagiarism or lack of originality (Lingard 2023), utilized ethically Chat￾GPT, like the software programs mentioned above, can support research processes 
such as qualitative data analysis as a powerful tool and further enhance their rigor. 
In a recent review of this AI-based tool for thematic analysis, Morgan (2023) notes 
that ChatGPT is successful in finding concrete, descriptive themes and points to its 
“substantial potential” (p. 9) to contribute to qualitative analysis. Accordingly, in the 
following section, we provide a six-step guide for conducting thematic analysis using 
ChatGPT, including examples of prompts and a dataset comprised of 30 interviews, 
in the hope that it will serve as a guide for future researchers interested in its use. The 
complete list of the prompts we used and the subsequent ChatGPT responses can be 
found in the Online Appendix. Each prompt number we indicate in the protocol cor￾responds to the prompt of the same number in Appendix A.

2  Thematic analysis with ChatGPT
To better illustrate the research protocol, a dataset comprising 30 semi-structured, in￾depth interviews was utilized. This dataset originated from a qualitative study that 
examined citizens’ use of social media, personality traits, and consumption of political 
content, as well as its influence on their political opinions. The primary aim of the study 
was to explore citizens’ awareness of the influence they experience on social media and 
to investigate the role their personality traits play in these processes. The study was 
guided by three research questions:
RQ1 What are the personality traits of users who change their political opinions on social 
media?
RQ2 What kind of political content influences social media users’ political opinions?
RQ3 On which topics do social media users tend to change their political opinions?
The interview guide was structured around three topic areas. The first one examined 
citizens’ use of social media as an information source for public affairs and politics. 
The second part examined personality traits and the openness of participants to opinion 
change. Finally, the third part inquired about citizens’ exposure to political content and 
discussions in social media, political opinion expression, and the influence that such 
political content may have on citizens’ political opinions. The interviews were tran￾scribed manually. The content addressed in this study mainly revolves around the use of 
social media to change citizens’ opinions.
2.1  Phase 1: Data preparation
Researchers should upload their textual data to ChatGPT following certain steps to 
ensure that the generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) processes it accurately. This 
process enables the AI to store the dataset, allowing researchers to make targeted que￾ries later. In addition, it saves time, as researchers do not need to immerse themselves 
in the data so thoroughly at this stage. Traditionally, thematic analysis requires substan￾tial time for researchers to familiarize themselves with the dataset through transcription, 
repeated reading, and iterative engagement with the text (Braun and Clarke 2006). By 
contrast, researchers utilizing ChatGPT only need to ensure that their data is transcribed 
beforehand, streamlining the early stages of analysis.
Accordingly, before uploading data to ChatGPT, it is essential to ensure that all 
information is consolidated within a single document, complete with appropriate head￾ings, labels, or questions. Once the data is properly organized, the next step is to access 
ChatGPT and provide an initial instruction (prompt) to contextualize the platform, spec￾ifying that a dataset will be uploaded (Prompt 1). Following this prompt, ChatGPT will 
register the interaction as a new chat, which will appear in the left-hand history panel of 
the user interface. Users may retain the default name assigned by ChatGPT or rename it 
according to their preference. It is recommended that researchers choose a project name 
that accurately reflects its content and enhances ease of identification.

The next step involves inputting the dataset—30 interviews in this case—into Chat￾GPT for analysis (Prompt 2). Due to the general length of qualitative data and the inher￾ent word count limitation of ChatGPT, it is advisable to load the material gradually to 
avoid potential errors in the GenAI system. Studies suggest that ChatGPT is prone to 
problems when processing inputs that exceed a certain number of words, even though 
there is no specific word limit defined (e.g., Lubiana et al. 2023). It is recommended to 
subdivide the interviews into 1000-word fragments to prevent technical issues, ensuring 
that each fragment is clearly labeled with the corresponding interviewee number. This 
will also enable the AI to analyze the set of interviews globally and recognize the origin 
of each answer, whether it belongs to interviewee 1, 2, and so forth, up to 30. With the 
release of GPT-4o, this limitation has been addressed, as the latest version allows for the 
direct upload of complete documents, such as full interview transcripts, which can then 
be analyzed without the need for manual segmentation.
Nevertheless, uploading the dataset per interviewee and subdividing it offers two key 
advantages. First, researchers can create prompts tailored to specific interviewees, allow￾ing for more precise query refinement. Second, ChatGPT can recognize that it is process￾ing a structured dataset composed of a specific number of interviews, ensuring that each 
response remains associated with the corresponding interviewee, thus minimizing potential 
errors in data processing and interpretation. Once all interviews have been integrated, it 
is essential to verify that ChatGPT has successfully loaded them by issuing a follow-up 
prompt (Prompt 3). After ChatGPT confirms that the content has been uploaded correctly, 
this phase is considered complete.
2.2  Phase 2: Defining the analysis process
The second phase of thematic analysis using ChatGPT focuses on identifying themes, 
patterns, or key concepts within the data. In manual analysis, researchers typically begin 
coding only after thoroughly familiarizing themselves with the dataset (Braun and Clarke 
2006; Maguire and Delahunt 2017; Vaismoradi et al. 2016). During this stage, they label 
data and make marginal annotations to identify potential patterns, gradually refining the 
dataset and preparing it for the subsequent phase: searching for themes that will ultimately 
form the foundation of the final report (Braun and Clarke 2006; Tuckett 2005). In thematic 
analysis conducted with ChatGPT, however, this sequence is reversed. The AI first identi￾fies overarching themes, and only in the next phase can researchers instruct it to display the 
specific codes or extracts associated with each theme. This inversion occurs because Chat￾GPT can detect patterns and derive themes without necessarily generating initial codes 
beforehand, streamlining the thematic analysis process.
To achieve this, OpenAI’s GenAI is instructed within the chat thread to identify com￾mon and recurring themes “as exhaustively as possible”, considering the entirety of the 
uploaded content (Prompt 5). This instruction enables ChatGPT to systematically track 
central themes within the dataset by recognizing patterns of repetition. As a result, Chat￾GPT provides a set of specific themes that can vary depending on the characteristics of the 
dataset (see Figures 1 and 2 in Online Appendix B for more detail). In our case, this results 
in twelve thematic axes around which we can structure the thematic analysis of our 30 
interviews (see Table 1). In addition, each theme contains some representative statements. 
It is also worth noting that researchers can ask ChatGPT to expand the themes found or 
specify the number of themes to display depending on their research interest.

After obtaining a general understanding of the central themes and keywords derived 
from the main topics of the interviews, the next step involves instructing ChatGPT to sum￾marize each of the identified themes individually. This process requires addressing the 
twelve themes separately, as requesting a summary of the entire dataset—comprising 30 
interviews—results in an error due to processing limitations. For instance, the first theme 
identified by ChatGPT was “Social Media Influence.” To summarize this theme, the fol￾lowing prompt was used: “Summarize the main points of the topic Social Media Influence 
identified in the previous prompt, using the dataset of 30 interviews” (Prompt 7).
The output provided a breakdown of the most important subtopics within this thematic 
axis and a summary for each subtopic. Additionally, ChatGPT generated an overview of 
the most significant elements from the interviews related to this theme.
Once the summaries are generated, they can be further analyzed by using ChatGPT to 
identify and list specific verbatims related to particular topics. To achieve targeted results, 
it is recommended to request “the best examples,” as this approach minimizes the likeli￾hood of receiving random or unrepresentative statements (Prompt 8). The output consists 
of specific quotes categorized by subtopics, which can be expanded through additional 
prompts if necessary. This process should be repeated for each of the identified topics—
twelve in this case—resulting in a comprehensive set of core statements. These statements 
provide a detailed overview of the most representative verbatims for each topic and their 
respective subtopics as identified by ChatGPT.

or patterns emerging from respondents’ answers, primarily encompassing areas of agree￾ment, while divergences highlight differences or contradictions in their responses, often 
providing nuanced insights. Second, this phase focuses on extracting direct quotes related 
to the primary and secondary themes identified in the previous phase. This process allows

2.4  Phase 4: Iterative process
In the fourth stage, researchers “define and refine” themes by engaging in an iterative con￾versation with the chatbot to acquire specific themes, associated keywords, and relevant 
excerpts from the identified thematic axes. This phase is crucial as it is during this stage 
that the AI will systematize the final outcome of the thematic analysis for each of the 12 
core axes obtained in the previous phase. Additionally, as in traditional thematic analysis, it 
helps researchers understand the essence of each theme and the story they want to tell with 
them (Braun and Clarke 2006).
To deepen the analysis, ChatGPT should be instructed to “perform a thematic analy￾sis” for each individual theme identified in the initial prompt (Prompt 13). For example, 
when the AI was asked to conduct a detailed thematic analysis for the theme “Social Media 
Influence,” it produced six key elements. First, the AI provided an overview of the topic, 
emphasizing the complex relationship between interviewees and social media. Second, it 
outlined key themes, such as the significance of social media as a primary source of infor￾mation, its influence on political opinions, its convenience and accessibility, and the trust 
and reliability of the information it offers. Third, the AI presented key terms associated 
with this central theme. Fourth, it analyzed convergences, which primarily revolved around 
the role of social media as a source of information, and divergences, which were mostly 
centered on trust in the information provided. Fifth, ChatGPT incorporated direct quotes 
from interviewees to illustrate specific examples of individual perspectives on social media 
use and its influence on opinions. Finally, the AI provided a conclusion and an overall sum￾mary of the findings.
Another major feature that adds systematization to the thematic analysis is a reasonable 
use of tables. Accordingly, at this stage, it is advisable to incorporate the following prompt:
Make a table where a thematic analysis is carried out on the “Social media influ￾ence” axis following all the steps proposed in the previous prompts. The chatbot 
should respond with identified topics, associated keywords, and relevant text excerpts 
(Prompt 14).
The result is a table that summarizes information on specific themes, associated key￾words, and relevant excerpts, providing a structured and clear overview of the thematic 
analysis conducted on the “Social Media Influence” topic (see Table 2 for more detail). 
In this case, ChatGPT mentions that the table incorporates steps from previous prompts, 
suggesting that this information is the cumulative result of a broader process. This table 
reflects a quick and accessible understanding of key aspects derived from the thematic 
analysis so far. After applying these two prompts to all thematic axes, the next step will be 
to carefully review ChatGPT’s thematic analysis.

to determine whether these findings can serve as a foundation for researchers seeking to 
extend the analysis through their own critical interpretation.
To check the reliability of the results obtained, we propose a three-step checklist using 
the most common methods to perform thematic analysis: (1) manually, (2) using CAQDAS 
software (NVivo), and (3) using an AI-powered CAQDAS (in this case, Atlas.ti’s AI Cod￾ing Beta Service). It is important to note that this checklist contains a set of core questions 
that can be adapted for different studies and outcomes within the thematic analysis meth￾odology. In this case, the checklist focuses on the thematic axis of Social Media Influence, 
specifically the results of prompts 13 and 14 (see Online Appendix A).
At the first level, critical thinking is applied through a series of questions on each the￾matic axis. Researchers are required to answer these control questions. In case of a nega￾tive result, an explanation and corrective action in the form of a new question is required. 
For the thematic axis of the influence of social media, the control questions are as follows:
Q1 The exploration of 30 interviews provides a rich tapestry of perspectives, revealing 
commonalities and distinctions in how people perceive and engage with these digital 
spaces as sources of information?
Q2 After reviewing the 30 interviews, can we confirm that the identified themes could be: 
primary information source, influence on political opinions, convenience and accessibility, 
trust and reliability, and filter bubbles and echo chambers?
Q3 Considering the responses of the interviewees, are the convergences and divergences 
proposed by ChatGPT correct?
Q4 Do the direct quotes provided by ChatGPT exist, and do they match the statements of 
the interviewees?
Q5 The keywords identified by ChatGPT accurately represent the statements of the 30 
interviewees in the thematic axis of social media influence?
After the critical examination of the questions for the 12 thematic axes, the qualitative 
analysis tool Nvivo (one of the most frequently used CAQDAS services for thematic analy￾sis) is used at the second control level. Specifically, two of its functions are used: (1) word 
frequency query and (2) word tree.
On the one hand, the word frequency query allows listing the words that occur most 
frequently in different choice sets. Therefore, in this task, researchers need to confirm 
whether, after uploading the 30 interviews to NVivo and using the “word frequency query” 
tool, they can obtain a tag cloud that can directly or at least partially represent the 12 axes 
identified by ChatGPT. On the other hand, a word tree is a visual representation, in the 

5502 M. Goyanes et al.
form of a graph, that helps identify frequent words and phrases within the data, demon￾strating how themes and insights have been derived. For illustration purposes, the keyword 
word tree will be applied to the theme “Social Media Influence.” This review task aims to 
assess whether the direct quotes obtained from the word tree for the selected thematic axis 
align with or are similar to those provided by ChatGPT. The word tree should be applied to 
each thematic axis to evaluate the consistency of the results generated by ChatGPT.
In the third and final phase of the inspection, the AI Coding Beta Tool of the CAQ￾DAS software Atlas.ti is used. This tool was developed to automate the coding of inter￾views through its AI-driven support. In this context, two questions are asked in the review 
checklist. The first question aims to determine whether, after uploading the 30 interviews 
to Atlas.ti and using the “AI Coding Beta” tool, any of the automatically generated codes 
match the key themes identified by ChatGPT. The second question aims to see if the codes 
generated by Atlas.ti use the same or similar key phrases or terms that we obtained through 
our prompts. These questions aim to assess the consistency of the results.
All checklist questions must be carefully analyzed by the co-authors. If, after analysis, 
the researchers can confirm that ChatGPT has given a correct answer, they should also 
justify why the result is correct. On the other hand, if the answer is incorrect or ambiguous, 
the co-authors should review earlier stages, refine the instructions or continue the conver￾sation to ensure the accuracy of the results.

3  Discussion and conclusions
In recent years, the emergence and development of language models as powerful tools 
for processing and generating human-like text has significantly transformed the experi￾ence of conducting both quantitative and qualitative data analysis (De Paoli and Mathis 
2024; Hariri 2023; Morgan 2023; Lubiana et al. 2023). In the case of qualitative research, 
artificial intelligence in general, and ChatGPT in particular, represent promising avenues 
to explore text transcriptions from interview data and facilitate the work of qualitative 
researchers (De Paoli and Mathis 2024; Morgan 2023). However, these promises have not 
yet been practically explored and empirically validated. This methodological paper is a 
step in this direction by providing a reliable research protocol for using ChatGPT and simi￾lar language models for qualitative data analysis.
Most notably, our paper puts forward a tested research protocol for thematic analysis 
with ChatGPT, which can be further tested and refined by other researchers. This protocol 
is based on the foundational work of Braun and Clarke (2006) being widely used by the 
qualitative research community around the globe (Byrne 2022; Forbes 2021; Javadi and 
Zarea 2016; Pilcher and Cortazzi 2024; Vaismoradi et al. 2016). That is, it is intended to 
resemble that original proposal, particularly in terms of the approach and spirit of data 
preparation, data analysis and data validation. Accordingly, this guideline attempts to cap￾ture the nuances of thematic analysis by proposing the following six steps: (1) data prepa￾ration, (2) defining the analysis process, (3) chatbot interaction, (4) iterative process, (5) 
review and validation, and (6) analysis and interpretation.
After the iterative process of data analysis, perhaps the hardest test for data validation is 
to examine the reliability of the proposed thematic patterns that emerge from the data. Our 
approach in this case was to test the reliability of the coding patterns using two other quali￾tative data analysis software programs, Atlas.ti and Nvivo, and then assess the reliability of 
the data using the checklist suggested in Table 3 and human intelligence. Any significant 
deviation from the results of the two software programs and the natural conclusions sug￾gested by human intelligence must be explained in detail and the next best course of action 
indicated. This design, which aims to evaluate the thematic patterns and verbatims emerg￾ing from ChatGPT using two independent qualitative data analysis software programs in 
addition to human intelligence, showcases the nature of this proposal and the transparent 
orientation of the data analysis.
The paper also offers theoretical implications, as it extends ongoing scholarly con￾versations about the use of AI and ChatGPT specifically in key aspects of research (e.g., 

Thematic analysis of interview data with ChatGPT: designing… 5507
De Paoli and Mathis 2024; Kumar 2023; Sallam 2023; Shtykalo and Yamnenko 2024). 
Although this AI tool is not the ultimate platform for qualitative data analysis (various soft￾ware packages have been successfully implemented in recent decades, such as Nvivo, Atlas.
ti, etc.), its powerful language model and its ability to summarize, interpret, and solve vari￾ous research problems make this artificial intelligence model an ideal candidate to assist 
researchers in many phases of empirical investigation (Morgan 2023). Thus, our study 
contributes to this body of research by providing a deeper understanding of the potential 
research uses of this tool while opening new avenues on the nuances of its applicability.
Similarly, this study contributes to the literature on advancements in qualitative data 
analysis (Castleberry and Nolen 2018; Hilal and Alabri 2013; Mohd Zabidi et al. 2022; 
O’Kane et al. 2021). This type of analysis is often described as one of the most challeng￾ing parts of qualitative research due to its time-consuming nature and the lack of explicit 
rules (Graue 2015; Hilal and Alabri 2013; Tuckett 2005). The ambiguity surrounding this 
process has also made it a target of criticism. Within this framework, the literature has 
been extensive in developing standardized models for coding documentary material with 
thematic analysis (see Maguire and Delahunt 2017), as well as in comparing quantitative 
and qualitative text analyses to extract codifications that distill the information contained in 
documents (Malandrino 2024). Similarly, prior research has been prolific in problematizing 
and refining thematic analysis, proposing frameworks to extend its scope and make it more 
reflective (Byrne 2022), or to clarify in detail its phases, nature, and practice (Finlay 2021). 
In this regard, the present study contributes to these discussions by proposing a protocol for 
thematic analysis assisted by artificial intelligence (see similar endeavors in Zhang et al. 
2023, 2024). This approach adheres to the guiding principles of traditional thematic analy￾sis while expanding the procedure to integrate new technological tools, ensuring both rigor 
and efficiency in the analysis process.
In sum, this study provides the literature and interested researchers with a reliable proto￾col with which to analyze textual data, successfully expanding the possibilities of qualita￾tive data analysis.
3.1  Limitations
In terms of limitations, we must acknowledge that ChatGPT is far from capable of replac￾ing the human skills required for qualitative research. While ChatGPT may provide an 
explicit but generally descriptive analysis of qualitative data, human intelligence remains 
essential for deriving more meaningful and implicit theoretical insights (De Paoli and 
Mathis 2024; Morgan 2023). Therefore, we highly recommend that researchers monitor 
and analyze the data alongside ChatGPT. Our examination of the platform in terms of the 
quality and granularity of thematic patterns reveals shortcomings, especially regarding the 
subtle nuances and contextual insights generally associated with qualitative research. To 
generate more granular insights with contextual meaning, the protocol needs to specify 
and design the prompt in detail, which may not make up for the time spent, as this could 
already be done by human intelligence. In short, perfecting the prompt is only worthwhile 
if the quality of the output, including nuance and contextual explanations, can improve the 
quality of human reasoning, which is difficult to achieve currently.
Finally, the ethos of qualitative research has always been linked to the creative pro￾cess derived from human intelligence. The standardization of research craft in qualitative 
research can improve the rigor and logical coherence of findings, but it can also suppress 
the metaphorical thinking and narrative craft of qualitative scholars. In essence, qualitative 

5508 M. Goyanes et al.
research is based on the hunches, highs and lows of researchers immersing themselves in 
the data and understanding the meaningful but sometimes subtle nuances of context and 
reflexivity. Summarizing the qualitative data analysis process may be appropriate for stud￾ies that focus on structure and description, but extending AI-powered qualitative analysis 
to all qualitative research could ultimately generate a more insipid, yet solid science.
