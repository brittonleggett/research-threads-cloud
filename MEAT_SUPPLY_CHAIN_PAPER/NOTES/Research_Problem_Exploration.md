## The question this memo answers
Does the brief's motivating tension — producers underpaid, consumers
overcharged, something in the middle capturing the difference — survive
contact with the evidence? Short answer: **not in the simple form it's
usually stated, but a real and more interesting puzzle survives underneath
it.**

## What does NOT hold up as stated

**"Farmers get almost nothing of the food dollar" — true for food in
general, false as a beef/pork-specific claim, and actively improving for
beef in the most recent data.** The oft-cited "~6 cents of the food dollar"
figure (5.9 cents in 2023, 5.8 cents in 2024, per USDA ERS Food Dollar
Series) describes the entire U.S. food basket — most of which is processed,
packaged, or restaurant food with a much larger labor/marketing/processing
component than raw meat. Using USDA ERS's own meat-specific "Meat Price
Spreads" series (a different, "standard animal" methodology), the beef farm
share of the *retail beef dollar* was 49.8 cents in 2023 and **rose** to
52.2 cents in 2024; pork farm share was 22.1 cents rising to 23.7 cents.
Conflating the two series — treating the whole-food-basket 6-cent figure as
if it describes beef specifically — is a real, identifiable factual error
common in advocacy discourse, and is itself a candidate finding for Study 1
(does corporate/producer-association discourse make or correct this
conflation?). See `LITERATURE/Price_Transmission_Literature.md` Section 2.

**"Imports are suppressing domestic cattle prices / lowering retail beef
prices" — contradicted by the most recent data, in the opposite direction
than usually assumed.** 2024 beef imports hit a record 4.635 billion lbs
(+24% year over year) at the *same time* the U.S. cattle herd fell to its
smallest size since 1951 and both cattle and retail beef prices hit record
highs. Imports and high prices rose together. The dominant driver appears to
be herd contraction (a domestic supply story — drought, cattle-cycle
economics), not import competition suppressing anything. This is close to
the reverse of the "cheap foreign beef is hurting American ranchers"
framing sometimes heard in the discourse, and equally the reverse of "more
imports means lower prices for consumers" — see
`ANALYSIS/Import_Structure_Beef_Pork_Poultry.md`.

**Pork and poultry don't fit the "producer squeezed, consumer gouged"
frame at all in the same way beef might.** Pork has been a net exporter
since 1995. Poultry imports are trivial (well under 1% of production) and
poultry is structurally not priced the way cattle/hogs are — see below.

## What DOES hold up, and is genuinely interesting

**Beef-packer concentration is a real, historically unusual structural
fact, not just a talking point.** CR4 for beef (steer/heifer) slaughter
rose from 36% (1980) to 80% (1997) to roughly 82% (~2021) across two
independent sources, one of them a primary USDA/ERS historical report
(AER-785, 2000) that itself calls this "no other product class shows as
dramatic an increase in any 15-year period" among roughly 1,000 Census
manufacturing classes tracked since 1947. That is a strong, well-sourced,
non-hyperbolic finding — it just isn't the same claim as "farmers get
nothing" or "imports are the problem." See
`LITERATURE/Market_Concentration_Evidence.md`.

**During acute supply shocks specifically (COVID-19, 2020), the farm-to-
retail spread widened and packer margins expanded — multiple independent
sources agree on this, for the shock period specifically.** This is
narrower and better-supported than a claim about "normal" period-to-period
price behavior; treat shock-period margin widening and baseline price-
transmission behavior as two separate empirical questions, not one. See
`LITERATURE/Price_Transmission_Literature.md` Section 4.

**Whether retail beef prices respond asymmetrically to farm-price
increases vs. decreases is a genuinely contested empirical question, not a
settled one — and the disagreement tracks a real methodological choice
(scanner/transaction data vs. BLS-derived average price series), not just
which side of a debate a source is on.** This matters for how confidently
the paper can lean on "price stickiness"/"rockets and feathers" language.

**Consumers currently cannot determine, from a standard label, whether beef
was born, raised, slaughtered, and processed in the U.S.** Mandatory COOL
for beef and pork was repealed in December 2015 (P.L. 114-113) after WTO
losses; the only current mechanism is a *voluntary* "Product of USA" claim,
recently tightened by USDA/FSIS (published March 2024, effective May 2024,
full compliance required January 2026) to require the full born/raised/
slaughtered/processed-in-U.S. chain for that specific voluntary claim. A
package without that voluntary claim discloses nothing about origin. This is
a real, verifiable, and probably under-appreciated-by-consumers information
gap — and it is squarely a transparency/labeling question, i.e., marketing
territory, not agricultural economics. See
`NOTES/COOL_Regulatory_Timeline.md`.

**Poultry cannot be analyzed with the beef/pork "farm price" framework at
all.** Broilers are raised almost entirely under contract with vertically
integrated companies, paid via a tournament system (relative performance
against other growers, not a market-clearing per-pound price). There is no
broiler equivalent of "the farm price of chicken." A 2024 USDA AMS rule
under the Packers and Stockyards Act directly regulates poultry grower
payment systems — itself evidence that USDA treats current grower-payment
structure as a live fairness issue. The "producer vs. consumer price
disconnect" question has to be reframed for poultry around grower-
compensation adequacy and integrator margin, not a farm price at all. See
`NOTES/Commodity_Structure_Comparison.md` and
`LITERATURE/Price_Transmission_Literature.md` Section 6.

## The puzzle that survives, restated
Not: *"corporations are gouging consumers and starving farmers, and imports/
concentration explain it."* That version doesn't survive contact with the
data (farm share is roughly half the beef dollar and rising; imports and
high prices moved together in 2024; pork/poultry don't fit the frame at
all).

Instead: **the real, evidence-backed puzzle is a gap between the structural
facts (beef-packer concentration is genuinely unusual by historical
standards; margins do widen during acute shocks; consumers cannot verify
origin from a label) and the much simpler, sometimes factually inverted,
story that circulates in public/political discourse about those facts
(conflating the whole food-dollar farm share with meat specifically;
assuming imports suppress prices when the two just moved together in the
same direction; assuming COOL still exists in something like its pre-2015
form).** That gap — between what's actually true and what gets said/
believed about it — is inherently a marketing/communication question:
*how do supply-chain actors describe this situation, and how does that
description shape what consumers believe and how they judge fairness,*
rather than *is the situation itself as bad as claimed.* This is consistent
with, and gives empirical teeth to, the transparency/attribution/price-
fairness theoretical framing already emerging as strongest in
`THEORY_CANDIDATES.md` and `NOTES/Marketing_Contribution_Options.md`.
