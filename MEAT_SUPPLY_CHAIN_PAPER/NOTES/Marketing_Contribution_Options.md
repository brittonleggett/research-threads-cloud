# Marketing Contribution Options (Section 14)

Status: first-pass ranking, 2026-09-03. This is the most important
strategic document in the project — everything else (Study 1 corpus scope,
Study 2 design, target journals) should follow from whichever story ranks
here, once `THEORY_CANDIDATES.md` (in progress) confirms which theoretical
lens actually has citation support. This draft ranks the brief's six
candidate angles on first-principles reasoning; revise once the theory
literature search returns.

## The core risk to manage
Everything about this topic (imports, concentration, farm-to-retail spreads)
is naturally described in agricultural-economics or industrial-organization
terms. The project fails as a *marketing* contribution if it just narrates
those facts with consumer-behavior vocabulary sprinkled on top. The
contribution has to be about how people (consumers, or the public more
broadly) *perceive, judge, and respond to* the supply chain — not about the
supply chain's mechanics themselves, which belong in the motivating/
background section, not the theoretical core.

## Ranked options

### 1. B — Supply-chain transparency (Strongest)
Consumers react differently when they know (or don't know) origin, producer
compensation, and processor/retailer margins. This is the angle with the
clearest, most distinctive theoretical hook: it treats *information about
value distribution*, not price itself, as the manipulated/measured variable.
It's also the angle best matched to a real, checkable regulatory hook (COOL
labeling limits — see `NOTES/COOL_Regulatory_Timeline.md`), which gives the
paper a concrete, citable institutional anchor rather than a purely
hypothetical scenario. Least redundant with existing COO-effects literature,
which has been worked heavily for decades; transparency-about-*distribution*
specifically (not just origin) is a thinner, more open literature.
**Best paired Study 2 design:** Option B in `STUDY2/Experiment_Options.md`.

### 2. D — Stakeholder distribution (Strong, largely overlaps with #1)
"Consumers may care not only about the price they pay but who captures the
value" is close to a restatement of the transparency angle's payoff, not a
separate contribution — the two should likely be treated as one combined
angle (transparency *of* distribution) rather than two papers' worth of
material. Keeping this as a distinct bullet mainly to flag that "distributive
justice" and "stakeholder theory" (see `THEORY_CANDIDATES.md`) are the two
literatures competing to anchor this same empirical idea, and one of them
should be picked, not both.

### 3. A — Price fairness and attribution (Strong, most directly tied to the
brief's core puzzle)
Consumers see high prices, farmers claim low returns, intermediaries explain
the gap. This is the most direct translation of the empirical puzzle into a
marketing question, and price-fairness/attribution theory is a well-
established, citable literature — lower novelty risk than B, but also lower
ceiling, since "corporate explanations affect perceived fairness" is a
well-worn finding pattern (need the meat-supply-chain-specific twist, e.g.
foreign-sourcing attribution specifically, to feel new).
**Best paired Study 2 design:** Option A in `STUDY2/Experiment_Options.md`.

### 4. C — Country-of-origin + price fairness (Possible, mainly as a
component of #1/#3 rather than standalone)
COO effects are one of the most heavily published literatures in
international marketing/consumer behavior — a COO-only framing risks feeling
incremental unless crossed with something less-published (which is exactly
what options A and B already do). Treat as an ingredient of the winning
combination, not a separate angle to rank on its own.

### 5. E — Corporate explanations for price increases (Possible, mainly as a
manipulated factor within #3, not a standalone story)
"How corporations justify price increases affects opportunism/fairness
perceptions" overlaps heavily with attribution theory (#3) and corporate-
opportunism literature already covered there. Fine as a manipulated factor
inside Option A of Study 2; weak as its own top-line contribution.

### 6. F — Consumer–producer alignment (Weakest as a standalone paper anchor)
"Consumers may prefer policies/firms perceived to protect both consumers and
producers simultaneously" is an interesting descriptive finding and a
plausible *outcome variable* (e.g., support for regulation, or a
dual-beneficiary framing condition) but is thin as a paper's central
theoretical contribution on its own — better used as one of Study 2's
outcome measures (already present in the brief's outcome list: "perceived
support for American farmers") than as the organizing frame.

## Recommendation
Lead with **Option B (transparency of value distribution)**, informed by
COO (Option C) as a moderator/manipulated factor and price-fairness/
attribution (Option A) as the mediating mechanism — i.e., transparency
about who captures value shapes attribution of responsibility for price
outcomes, which shapes perceived fairness/trust/opportunism, which shapes
downstream outcomes (WTP, purchase intention, support for regulation). This
is a chain+moderator structure, consistent with the pattern already
established in Britton's prior published work (see
`Claude_Knowledge/` — user_research_corpus notes a validated chain+moderator
theory pattern across his prior papers) rather than a novel structural
choice for this project specifically.
