# Theory Candidates

Status: draft, 2026-09-03. Exploratory literature/novelty search (Section 9 of
the project brief). Every seminal/recent citation below was checked against
search results and/or publisher/repository pages before inclusion; none were
recalled from memory without that check. This is a menu of options, not a
recommendation to lock a single theory — see the ranking rationale at the end
of each entry, and the overlap notes in the summary table.

Verdict scale: **Strong candidate** / **Possible** / **Weak or redundant**.

---

## 1. Channel power / supply-chain power — Strong candidate
- Seminal: Hunt, S.D. & Nevin, J.R. (1974). "Power in a Channel of
  Distribution: Sources and Consequences." *Journal of Marketing Research*,
  11(2), 186–193.
- Recent: channel-power framing continues to anchor food-supply-chain power
  studies in agribusiness/marketing journals (see also the resource-
  dependence and TCE entries below, which are frequently paired with it).
- Applied to food/ag: extensively — this is the natural lens for
  processor/retailer buyer power over farmers, a live topic in USDA/DOJ
  Packers and Stockyards Act discourse.
- Beyond ag-econ: yes — channel power is a marketing-channels construct, not
  an economics one; it asks about *behavioral* consequences (dependence,
  conflict, use of power sources) rather than just price outcomes.
- Marketing-journal legitimacy: yes — a core marketing-channels theory,
  publishable in JM/JMR-adjacent outlets and marketing-channels-focused
  journals.
- **Why Strong:** Directly explains the "why does one party feel it isn't
  getting its share" puzzle motivating this project, is a native marketing
  construct (not borrowed wholesale from ag-econ), and gives a clean home for
  concentration/monopsony evidence (Section 5) as an *antecedent* of
  perceived power rather than the study's whole contribution.

## 2. Resource dependence theory — Possible
- Seminal: Pfeffer, J. & Salancik, G.R. (1978). *The External Control of
  Organizations: A Resource Dependence Perspective.* Harper & Row.
- Applied to food/ag: used in agribusiness/supply-chain-management research
  on grower contracting (esp. poultry tournament contracts) and vertical
  integration.
- Beyond ag-econ: partially — RDT is organization-theory/strategy, not
  ag-econ, but it's firm-level, not consumer-level, so it pulls the paper
  toward management rather than marketing unless paired with a
  consumer-facing outcome.
- Marketing-journal legitimacy: weaker on its own; strong as a *structural
  antecedent* chapter feeding into a consumer-perception DV (fits the "chain
  + moderator" pattern noted in Britton's other work — see summary table).
- **Why Possible, not Strong:** Good for explaining grower/processor
  dependence structurally (esp. poultry), but doesn't by itself generate a
  marketing-journal-worthy consumer-side contribution.

## 3. Transaction cost economics (TCE) — Weak/redundant for this project
- Seminal: Williamson, O.E. (1985). *The Economic Institutions of
  Capitalism: Firms, Markets, Relational Contracting.* Free Press.
- Applied to food/ag: heavily used to explain vertical integration in
  poultry/pork (asset specificity of live-animal production justifying
  contract/ownership structures).
- Beyond ag-econ: no — TCE **is** the dominant ag-econ/IO explanation for
  vertical integration in meat already; using it here would mostly
  re-derive what agricultural economists already established.
- Marketing-journal legitimacy: low as a lead theory; fine as background
  citation explaining *why* poultry is vertically integrated and beef/pork
  packing is not (to the same degree), which matters for
  `NOTES/Commodity_Structure_Comparison.md`, but not a viable anchor
  construct for a marketing contribution.
- **Why Weak here specifically:** Necessary background, not a differentiator.

## 4. Distributive justice / equity theory — Possible, likely redundant with price fairness (see below)
- Seminal: Adams, J.S. (1965). "Inequity in Social Exchange." *Advances in
  Experimental Social Psychology*, 2, 267–299.
- Applied to food/ag: underlies "farmers aren't getting their fair share"
  framing broadly, but rarely operationalized directly in food-marketing
  studies — usually absorbed into price-fairness or organizational-justice
  measures instead.
- Beyond ag-econ / marketing legitimacy: yes in principle (equity theory is
  psychology, well-used in marketing), but in practice #5 (price fairness)
  is the more common, more directly citable operationalization in top
  marketing journals for exactly this kind of "who gets what share" question.
- **Why Possible, not Strong:** Real theoretical grounding, but redundant
  with price fairness (#5) as the anchor construct — cite Adams as the
  psychological foundation *under* a price-fairness or distributive-fairness
  measure rather than as a stand-alone lens.

## 5. Price fairness — Strong candidate
- Seminal: Xia, L., Monroe, K.B., & Cox, J.L. (2004). "The Price Is Unfair!
  A Conceptual Framework of Price Fairness Perceptions." *Journal of
  Marketing*, 68(4), 1–15. Also foundational: Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J.L., &
  Thaler, R. (1986). "Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking:
  Entitlements in the Market." *American Economic Review*, 76(4), 728–741
  (the "acceptable to raise prices when costs rise, unfair to raise prices
  when demand shifts" finding is directly on point for meat-price framing).
- Recent/domain-relevant: farmdoc daily (Univ. of Illinois, 2024), "Fair
  Fare? US Consumers' Perceptions of Food Pricing 'Fairness' in Restaurants
  and Grocery Stores" — a live, food-specific application, though this is an
  extension/outreach piece, not peer-reviewed; useful as a pointer to the
  gap, not as a theory citation.
- Applied to food/ag: growing, esp. post-2021-23 inflation cycle, but still
  thin in top marketing journals specifically on meat/grocery.
- Beyond ag-econ / marketing legitimacy: yes — this is a marketing-native
  construct with a large validated measurement tradition (dual entitlement,
  reference-price violations, cost- vs. demand-based justification effects
  from Kahneman et al. map directly onto "processors raised prices citing
  input costs vs. raised prices when demand was strong" scenarios).
- **Why Strong:** This is probably the single best-fitting anchor DV or
  mediator for a consumer-facing Study 2 — corporate explanation (cost-based
  vs. margin-based vs. import-competition-based) is a near-literal
  replication-extension of the Kahneman et al. dual-entitlement logic, applied
  to a commodity (meat) that has genuine, verifiable cost-shock variation.

## 6. Attribution theory — Strong candidate
- Seminal (consumer behavior application): Folkes, V.S. (1988). "Recent
  Attribution Research in Consumer Behavior: A Review and New Directions."
  *Journal of Consumer Research*, 14(4), 548–565. (Underlying psychological
  theory: Weiner's attributional framework — not separately re-verified this
  session; well-established and citable via Folkes' review and subsequent
  marketing literature.)
- Applied to food/ag: this is essentially what Section 10's Study 1 research
  question already assumes ("how do supply-chain actors attribute
  responsibility...") — attribution theory is the formal name for that
  mechanism.
- Beyond ag-econ: yes, purely a consumer-psychology/marketing construct.
- Marketing-journal legitimacy: yes, well-established.
- **Why Strong:** Directly names the Study 1 coding logic (input-cost
  attribution, foreign-import attribution, retailer attribution, etc., as
  listed in the brief, are literally attribution categories) and connects
  cleanly to price fairness (#5) as antecedent→mediator.

## 7. Perceived price gouging — Possible, likely folds into price fairness
- Seminal: Campbell, M.C. (1999). "Perceptions of Price Unfairness:
  Antecedents and Consequences." *Journal of Marketing Research*, 36(2),
  187–199. (Inferred seller motive — profit-seeking vs. cost-driven — as the
  key driver of unfairness perception.)
- **Why Possible, not Strong on its own:** This is essentially price
  fairness (#5) with "motive attribution" (#6) built in — it's the natural
  intersection of #5 and #6 rather than a distinct third lens. Treat
  "perceived price gouging/opportunism" as the outcome label, with price
  fairness + attribution as the theoretical machinery producing it.

## 8. Consumer inference — Weak/redundant as a standalone lens
No single seminal citation was pursued separately; consumer-inference-making
is the general process underlying attribution theory (#6) and price fairness
(#5) judgments (e.g., inferring seller motive from a price change, inferring
product quality from country of origin). **Recommend folding into #5/#6/#12
rather than treating as its own construct** — listing it separately would
likely read as padding to a reviewer.

## 9. Corporate opportunism — Possible
- Seminal: John, G. (1984). "An Empirical Investigation of Some Antecedents
  of Opportunism in a Marketing Channel." *Journal of Marketing Research*,
  21(3), 278–289.
- Applied to food/ag: opportunism is a standard channel-relationships
  construct (see also Brown, Dev, & Lee 2000, *Journal of Marketing*, on
  governance mechanisms curbing channel opportunism), not previously applied
  much to meat-industry consumer perception specifically.
- **Why Possible:** Good as a *label* for what consumers might perceive
  (an outcome construct, like #7), and useful if the paper takes a B2B
  supply-chain-relationships angle (processor↔packer↔retailer) rather than a
  pure consumer angle. Less central if the paper stays consumer-facing.

## 10. Institutional trust — Strong candidate
- Seminal: Mayer, R.C., Davis, J.H., & Schoorman, F.D. (1995). "An
  Integrative Model of Organizational Trust." *Academy of Management
  Review*, 20(3), 709–734. (Ability/benevolence/integrity trustworthiness
  model.)
- Applied to food/ag: directly relevant to whether consumers trust USDA
  labeling claims, processor statements about sourcing, or "Product of USA"
  claims specifically — trust-in-institution is a live, testable DV.
- Beyond ag-econ / marketing legitimacy: yes, core to consumer-trust
  marketing literature and food-safety/food-policy trust research.
- **Why Strong:** The ability/benevolence/integrity structure maps cleanly
  onto "does this processor have the competence, the good intent toward
  farmers/consumers, and the honesty in labeling" — a natural outcome for a
  transparency manipulation (see #14/#15 below) and complements price
  fairness as a second DV.

## 11. Food provenance — Weak/redundant as a standalone lens
- Best available anchor found: "Indigenous Meanings of Provenance in the
  Context of Alternative Food Movements and Supply-Chain Traceability: A
  Review," *Land* (MDPI), 2021 — a review, not a single foundational
  citation the way COO effects or traceability have one.
- **Why Weak/redundant:** No search turned up a clean seminal
  marketing-journal citation distinct from country-of-origin effects (#12)
  and traceability (#13); "provenance" in the literature is mostly used as a
  qualitative/wine-and-craft-branding umbrella term that overlaps almost
  entirely with COO + traceability + authenticity. Recommend dropping as a
  separate lens and using COO effects (#12) as the operational construct
  instead.

## 12. Consumer ethnocentrism — Strong candidate
- Seminal: Shimp, T.A. & Sharma, S. (1987). "Consumer Ethnocentrism:
  Construction and Validation of the CETSCALE." *Journal of Marketing
  Research*, 24(3), 280–289.
- Recent/domain-relevant: Gao, Z. et al. (2024). "Explaining consumer
  willingness to pay for country-of-origin labeling with ethnocentrism,
  country image, and product image: Examples from China's beef market."
  *Canadian Journal of Agricultural Economics*, — a discrete-choice
  experiment finding ethnocentrism increases WTP for domestic beef and
  discounts foreign beef; near-identical design logic to what Section 11's
  Study 2 options describe, just in a different country. Directly citable
  as both prior art and a design template to differentiate from.
- Beyond ag-econ: yes, native marketing/international-business construct.
- Marketing-journal legitimacy: yes, validated scale (CETSCALE), extremely
  well-cited (2000+ citations), still actively used.
- **Why Strong:** CETSCALE is a validated, off-the-shelf moderator — testing
  whether ethnocentrism moderates reactions to import-related price framing
  (a treatment this project can actually manipulate) is a clean, well-
  precedented design.

## 13. Country-of-origin (COO) effects — Strong candidate
- Seminal: Bilkey, W.J. & Nes, E. (1982). "Country-of-Origin Effects on
  Product Evaluations." *Journal of International Business Studies*, 13(1),
  89–100.
- Meta-analytic anchor: Verlegh, P.W.J. & Steenkamp, J.-B.E.M. (1999). "A
  Review and Meta-Analysis of Country-of-Origin Research." *Journal of
  Economic Psychology*, 20(5), 521–546 — finds COO affects perceived quality
  more strongly than attitude/purchase intention, a useful calibration for
  what outcome variables to expect movement on.
- Applied to food/ag: extensively, including meat specifically (Gao et al.
  2024 above; also long-standing EU PDO/PGI-labeling literature).
- **Why Strong:** COO is the most directly on-topic, best-validated
  construct in the entire list given the project's explicit interest in
  foreign sourcing and "Product of USA" labeling. Recommend this as the
  primary construct over #11 (provenance) and paired with #12
  (ethnocentrism) as moderator.

## 14. Transparency — Strong candidate
- Anchor: Kraft, T., Valdés, L., & Zheng, Y. (2022). "Consumer Trust in
  Social Responsibility Communications: The Role of Supply Chain
  Visibility." *Production and Operations Management*, 31(12) — supply-chain
  visibility → consumer trust, published in a strong operations/marketing-
  adjacent outlet.
- Related recent synthesis: "Disclosing transparency: A review of B2B
  marketing and supply chain research," *Industrial Marketing Management*
  (2024) — notes signaling theory (#16 below) as the usual mechanism linking
  transparency disclosures to trust.
- Applied to food/ag: growing fast, directly relevant to the "detailed
  supply-chain transparency vs. vague vs. none" manipulation Section 11
  already proposes.
- **Why Strong:** This is the most directly manipulable construct for Study
  2 (transparency about origin/margins is literally a treatment condition
  the brief proposes) and pairs naturally with institutional trust (#10) as
  the DV and signaling theory (#16) as the mechanism.

## 15. Traceability — Possible, largely subsumed under transparency (#14)
- Seminal (economics/policy side): Golan, E., Krissoff, B., Kuchler, F.,
  Calvin, L., Nelson, K., & Price, G. (2004). *Traceability in the U.S. Food
  Supply: Economic Theory and Industry Studies.* USDA Economic Research
  Service, Agricultural Economic Report No. 830.
- **Why Possible, not Strong:** Traceability is the *operational/technical*
  capability (can the supply chain actually track an animal's path);
  transparency (#14) is the *consumer-facing disclosure* of that capability.
  For a marketing paper, transparency is the more citable, more consumer-
  psychology-native construct; traceability belongs in the background/
  regulatory context (feeds `NOTES/COOL_Regulatory_Timeline.md`) rather than
  as a headline theory.

## 16. Stakeholder theory — Possible
- Seminal: Freeman, R.E. (1984). *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder
  Approach.* Pitman.
- Applied to food/ag: used broadly in CSR/sustainability food-supply-chain
  research; less common as a consumer-cognition lens (it's usually a firm-
  strategy or normative-ethics frame).
- **Why Possible, not Strong:** Useful framing device for Section 14's "who
  captures the value" angle (farmer vs. processor vs. retailer vs. importer
  vs. consumer as competing stakeholders) but thin as a testable
  psychological mechanism on its own — better as a narrative frame in the
  introduction than as the paper's theoretical engine.

## 17. Signaling theory — Strong candidate (added; not in the brief's list, but directly relevant and already used in the sister CCS_PAPER project)
- Seminal: Spence, M. (1973). "Job Market Signaling." *Quarterly Journal of
  Economics*, 87(3), 355–374.
- Modern management/marketing review: Connelly, B.L., Certo, S.T., Ireland,
  R.D., & Reutzel, C.R. (2011). "Signaling Theory: A Review and Assessment."
  *Journal of Management*, 37(1), 39–67.
- Applied to food/ag: used for eco-label/certification signaling; the
  Industrial Marketing Management 2024 transparency review (#14) explicitly
  flags signaling theory as the standard mechanism for supply-chain-
  disclosure-to-trust effects.
- **Why Strong:** Gives a formal information-asymmetry mechanism for *why*
  a "Product of USA" claim, a transparency disclosure, or a corporate price-
  increase explanation should move consumer trust/fairness judgments at all
  (sender has private information the receiver can't otherwise verify) —
  this is the same construct doing work in `CCS_PAPER`'s governance-frame
  design, so there's an internal methodological precedent to draw on.

## Most promising combination for a marketing-journal contribution
**Country-of-origin disclosure + corporate price-increase explanation
(cost-based vs. margin-based vs. import-competition-based) →
[price fairness / attributed motive] → [institutional trust, purchase
intention] , moderated by consumer ethnocentrism**, with signaling theory as
the mechanism explaining why transparency/disclosure moves the mediators at
all, and channel power / resource dependence held in reserve as the
*structural, evidence-based justification* (from Sections 5–8's empirical
work) for why such disclosures would or wouldn't be informative in the first
place. This mirrors the chain-plus-moderator pattern already used elsewhere
in Britton's research program (see `user_research_corpus.md` memory note) —
worth confirming that pattern's specifics against his actual published papers
before committing, rather than assuming from this session alone.

This is a menu, not a decision — Section 11 (`STUDY2/Experiment_Options.md`)
will translate 2–4 concrete design options for Britton to choose among.

## Addendum (2026-09-03, added after initial draft): a direct beef-price-
fairness precedent

Independently found and verified (WebSearch + cross-check, not from the same
research pass that produced the rest of this file): Sun, K.-A. & Moon, J.
(2025). "Exploring the Antecedents and Consequences of Perceived Fairness in
Beef Pricing: The Moderating Role of Freshness Under Conditions of
Information Overload." *Foods*, 14(11), 1844. DOI: 10.3390/foods14111844.
415 U.S. consumer responses (Clickworker panel); examines organic
perception → price fairness → revisit intention with freshness as a
moderator. This is directly on-topic for construct #5 (price fairness) —
it's a live, 2025, U.S.-consumer, beef-specific price-fairness study, which
both (a) confirms this construct has current traction for exactly this
commodity and (b) is a paper this project will need to differentiate from
explicitly (its mechanism is organic-labeling/freshness, not origin/
transparency/attribution, so the novelty gap is real but should be stated
directly in the lit review rather than left implicit). Also relevant: Gao,
Z. et al. (2024, cited above at #12) on COO/ethnocentrism/WTP in China's beef
market is the closer precedent for the COO+ethnocentrism pairing
specifically. Between the two, no directly on-topic U.S. paper yet combines
transparency-of-distribution + attribution + price fairness for meat
specifically — that gap is what
`NOTES/Marketing_Contribution_Options.md` is betting the project's
contribution on.
