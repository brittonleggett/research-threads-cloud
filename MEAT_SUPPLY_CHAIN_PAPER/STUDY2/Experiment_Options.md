# Study 2 — Experiment Options (Section 11)

Status: options memo, 2026-09-03. NOT a final design. Britton chooses; this
just narrows the brief's four manipulation dimensions (country of origin,
corporate explanation, transparency, benefit distribution) and long outcome
list into 2–4 credible, publishable-scale designs, ranked. Full factorial
crossing of all four manipulations (up to 4×6×3×3 cells in the brief's raw
list) is not viable for a single study — needs trimming regardless of which
option is chosen.

Grounding: whatever design is chosen should be informed by which theoretical
lens(es) come out "Strong candidate" in `THEORY_CANDIDATES.md` and by what
Study 1 actually finds emerges as the dominant attribution/justification
pattern in real corporate discourse — vignette conditions should mirror real
message types where possible (same logic TARIFF_PAPER used: Study 1 typology
grounds realistic Study 2 vignette stimuli), not be invented independently of
the corpus.

## Option A — Country of Origin × Corporate Price Explanation (2×3 or 2×4)
**Design:** Origin (U.S.-raised vs. imported, collapse the 4-level brief list
to the cleanest 2-cell contrast, or add a 3rd "origin not disclosed" cell if
that maps onto a real Study 1 finding) crossed with corporate explanation for
price (cost-based/supply-chain-cost vs. margin/profit-based vs. foreign-
competition-based vs. no explanation — trim to 3 of these 4 based on Study 1
frequency).
**Outcomes:** perceived price fairness, trust, perceived corporate
opportunism, purchase intention.
**Strongest fit if:** price-fairness/attribution theory and country-of-origin
effects both rank Strong in `THEORY_CANDIDATES.md`. This is the design most
directly targeted at the brief's core empirical puzzle (producer-consumer
price disconnect) filtered through a marketing lens.
**Risk:** COO and corporate-explanation are two well-trodden literatures
separately; the contribution rests entirely on the *interaction* being
theoretically novel and empirically clean — needs a clear a priori
hypothesis for why they interact, not just "let's cross two factors."

## Option B — Transparency × Distribution of Benefits (2×3 or 3×3)
**Design:** Supply-chain transparency (detailed vs. vague vs. none) crossed
with where value goes (lower consumer price vs. higher farmer pay vs. higher
corporate margin).
**Outcomes:** perceived fairness, trust, willingness to pay, support for
COOL-style regulation.
**Strongest fit if:** transparency/traceability and stakeholder-distribution
concerns rank Strong. This design most directly operationalizes the brief's
"consumers may care not only about the price they pay but who captures the
value" framing (Section 14D) — arguably the single most distinctive,
least-already-published angle of the four options here.
**Risk:** "distribution of benefits" as a between-subjects manipulation is
somewhat artificial/hypothetical for a consumer (real supply chains don't
let a shopper choose where the margin goes) — needs a realistic cover story
(e.g., framed as a hypothetical new labeling program) to avoid feeling like
a forced-choice abstraction rather than a believable stimulus.

## Option C — Single-factor Country-of-Origin-Label-Meaning Manipulation
**Design:** A simpler single-factor design manipulating *what a "Product of
USA" style label actually discloses* (full traceability claim vs. the
current USDA voluntary-label reality vs. a fully undisclosed-origin control),
informed directly by whatever `NOTES/COOL_Regulatory_Timeline.md` finds about
what current labels can legally claim.
**Outcomes:** perceived authenticity, perceived support for American
farmers, perceived deception/opportunism, willingness to pay, support for
mandatory COOL-style regulation.
**Strongest fit if:** the COOL fact-check work turns up a genuinely
surprising gap between what consumers likely assume a label means and what
it's legally allowed to mean — that gap itself is the "unexplained
phenomenon" a theory needs to address (naturally pairs with consumer
inference / corporate opportunism / institutional trust).
**Risk:** narrower single-factor design is easier to run cleanly and
publish, but a lower ceiling on theoretical contribution unless the
consumer-inference angle is unusually strong — most likely a fallback option
if A or B prove too complex, not a first choice.

## Option D — Full 2×2: Country of Origin × Transparency (drop explanation
and benefit-distribution factors to a covariate/exploratory measure)
**Design:** Origin (domestic vs. imported) × Transparency (disclosed
supply-chain detail vs. not), the two factors most central to the brief's COO
+ transparency framing (Sections 14B/14C combined), with corporate
explanation type and benefit distribution measured as exploratory/covariate
items rather than manipulated.
**Outcomes:** perceived price fairness, trust, purchase intention, WTP,
support for COOL regulation.
**Strongest fit if:** Britton wants the cleanest, most conventional 2×2 that
a reviewer will find easy to evaluate, at some cost to novelty relative to
Option B.
**Risk:** most "textbook" of the four, least distinctive contribution — safe
but not the strongest theoretical story on its own.

## Ranking (tentative, pending Study 1 findings and THEORY_CANDIDATES.md)
1. **Option B** (transparency × benefit distribution) — most novel framing,
   most directly answers "why would a marketing journal care about this,"
   least redundant with existing COO-effects literature.
2. **Option A** (COO × corporate explanation) — most directly tied to the
   brief's core empirical puzzle; strong fallback if Option B's benefit-
   distribution manipulation proves too artificial in pretesting.
3. **Option D** (COO × transparency 2×2) — safest, most conventional; good
   fallback if reviewers of an early draft want a simpler design.
4. **Option C** (single-factor label-meaning) — best only if the COOL
   fact-check surfaces an unusually sharp consumer-belief/legal-reality gap;
   otherwise the weakest standalone contribution of the four.

## Explicit non-decision
No option is selected. This ranking is a starting recommendation for
discussion, not a lock — per project research standards, study-design
decisions with multiple defensible choices are Britton's call.
