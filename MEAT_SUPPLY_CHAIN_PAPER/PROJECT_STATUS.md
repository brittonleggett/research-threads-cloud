## What we currently know

- **Beef-packer concentration is a real, historically unusual structural
  fact, though the most current officially-sourced data shows it flat-to-
  slightly-declining since 2019, not still rising.** Steer/heifer CR4 rose
  36% (1980) → 80% (1997) → 85% (2019) → **81% (2020-2021, per USDA AMS
  Packers and Stockyards Division's own Annual Report to Congress, read
  directly 2026-09-05)**, with the recent dip attributed by the agency to
  COVID-19 plant disruptions rather than genuine deconcentration. The
  1963-1997 rise is a primary USDA/ERS historical finding (AER-785) that
  itself calls it unique among ~1,000 Census product classes tracked since
  1947. Pork CR4: 34%→54%→67% (2019)→65% (2021). **Poultry: 32%→41%→53-55%
  (2019-2021, per the same PSD report)** — revised down from an earlier,
  now-contradicted "78% (2019)" figure that traced to an unread, paywalled
  source (see Open Decisions #6); poultry's rise remains, on this
  corrected figure, the most modest of the three commodities.
- **The commodities are structurally distinct and should not be pooled.**
  Beef is still substantially open/cash market with partial "captive
  supply." Pork shifted sharply toward contract/direct-ownership since the
  1990s (~2% to ~69% under contract, 1980→2004). Poultry is ~99.5% under
  production contract with a tournament-style relative-pay system —
  growers don't own the birds and there is no broiler equivalent of a "farm
  price." See `NOTES/Commodity_Structure_Comparison.md`.
- **Beef farm share of the retail dollar is ~50% and rose in the most
  recent year available (49.8¢→52.2¢, 2023→2024), not the ~6¢ figure
  commonly (and wrongly) applied to meat.** The "~6 cents of the food
  dollar" statistic is real but describes the entire U.S. food basket, not
  meat specifically. Pork farm share also rose (22.1¢→23.7¢).
- **2024 U.S. beef imports hit a record (4.635B lbs, +24% YoY) at the same
  time the U.S. cattle herd hit its smallest size since 1951 and cattle/
  beef prices hit records — imports and high prices rose together.** The
  dominant driver appears to be domestic herd contraction, not import
  competition. Ground-beef lean-trim blending with imported beef is
  directly confirmed by USDA ERS. Pork has been a net exporter since 1995;
  poultry imports are trivial (well under 1% of production).
- **Consumers cannot verify domestic origin from a standard meat label.**
  Mandatory COOL for beef/pork was repealed Dec. 2015 (P.L. 114-113) after
  a WTO loss to Canada/Mexico. The only current mechanism is a *voluntary*
  "Product of USA" claim, recently tightened (published March 2024,
  effective May 2024, full compliance required Jan. 1, 2026) to require the
  full born/raised/slaughtered/processed-in-U.S. chain. Mandatory COOL
  persists for several other commodities (lamb, chicken, seafood, etc.).
- **During the acute 2020-2021 COVID plant-disruption shock specifically,
  packer margins widened and the farm-retail spread grew, for beef and pork
  specifically — not clearly for poultry.** Three independent peer-reviewed/
  USDA sources triangulate on the price-spread evidence, and as of
  2026-09-05 this is now also directly confirmed by Tyson's and JBS's own
  audited SEC filings: Tyson's Beef segment operating margin rose from 6.6%
  (FY2019) to 18.0% (FY2021), with Tyson's own 10-K attributing the rise to
  COVID-19 widening the spread between contract prices and cattle costs;
  JBS's Beef North America segment shows the same pattern independently
  (~13.1%→~21.3%, 2020-2021). Margins reverted sharply afterward (Tyson Beef
  fell to a loss by FY2023). Poultry did not show the same pattern at either
  company. This is well-supported for that shock episode and those two
  commodities specifically; it is not established as a general "disruptions
  always benefit processors" rule, and should not be extended to poultry
  without separate evidence.
- **Asymmetric price transmission ("rockets and feathers") in beef is
  genuinely contested in the literature, and the disagreement tracks
  methodology** — older/BLS-price-based studies find it; the most
  methodologically current beef study (using actual retail scanner data)
  finds none.

## What appears interesting (the empirical puzzle that survived fact-checking)

Not the naive version — *"corporations gouge consumers and starve
farmers, and imports/concentration are why."* That doesn't survive contact
with the data. Instead: **a gap between real structural facts (beef-packer
concentration genuinely is a historical outlier; margins do widen during
acute shocks; consumers genuinely can't verify origin from a label) and a
simpler, sometimes factually inverted, popular narrative about those facts**
(conflating the whole-food-dollar farm share with meat specifically;
assuming imports suppress prices when the two just moved together;
assuming COOL still functions as it did pre-2015). That gap is a
communication/attribution question, not a "how bad is the situation"
question — see `NOTES/Research_Problem_Exploration.md` for the full
argument. This reframing is what makes the project a genuine marketing
contribution rather than an agricultural-economics restatement.

## What did NOT hold up

- "Farmers get almost nothing of the food dollar" — true for food overall,
  false as a beef/pork-specific claim, and the beef figure *rose* in 2024.
- "Foreign imports are lowering/suppressing beef prices for consumers or
  ranchers" — contradicted by the 2024 data (imports and prices rose
  together); verdict: Misleading without context / Unresolved.
- "COOL still protects consumers the way it did before" — repealed for
  beef/pork over a decade ago (2015); most consumers likely don't know
  this.
- "Food monopolies" as a technically accurate description — no sector shows
  single-firm monopoly; "oligopoly"/"concentrated"/"monopsony concern" are
  the defensible terms. Useful finding in its own right (a framing gap
  Study 1 could examine), not a factual claim to adopt.

  brief claims scored).

## Best marketing contribution (current recommendation)

Lead with **transparency of value distribution** — do consumers react
differently when they know (or don't know) who captures value across the
chain (farmer, processor, retailer, importer)? — informed by
**country-of-origin disclosure** as a moderator/manipulated factor and
**price-fairness/attribution theory** as the mediating mechanism: COO
disclosure + corporate price-explanation type → attributed motive/price
fairness → institutional trust/purchase intention, moderated by consumer
ethnocentrism, with **signaling theory** as the mechanism explaining why
disclosure moves the mediators at all, and **channel power/concentration**
evidence (Sections 3/5 above) held in reserve as the structural
justification for why such disclosures would be informative in the first
place. This is a chain+moderator structure consistent with the pattern
already used in Britton's prior published work. Full ranking of six
candidate angles: `NOTES/Marketing_Contribution_Options.md`. Full theory
literature review (17 lenses, ranked, with verified citations):
`THEORY_CANDIDATES.md`. **2026-09-15 gap-check** (targeted, not systematic — see
`LITERATURE/Consumer_Marketing_Literature_Scan_2026-09-15.md`): two 2025 U.S. beef/price-fairness
studies exist (Sun & Moon, *Foods* and *Nutrients*) but in food-science journals with an
organic/freshness framing, not COO/corporate-explanation; one 2024 marketing-journal
transparency→price-fairness study exists (Casteran, *Recherche et Applications en Marketing*) but
for general retail/Fairtrade, not meat. This project's specific combination — COO disclosure ×
corporate price-explanation type in a meat context, in a marketing journal — still reads as an
open gap on this evidence, cautiously not definitively.
